[1] Moreover, although you may furnish a king with wisdom, great power, or the highest authority, he will accomplish nothing successfully, and his kingdom will be neither firm nor stable, unless he cultivates justice and administers law by its rules, without respect of persons. The wise Solomon himself observed this when he said: "Take away the wicked from before the king, and his throne will be established in righteousness." Likewise: "When the righteous rule, the people rejoice." And again: "The king establishes the land by judgment, but he who greedily seeks tributes and exactions overthrows it." "The king who judges the poor in truth, his throne will be established for ever." All these things will be understood to have been most truly said by those who have greeted histories from their very threshold, or who observe attentively what happens every day. For those who judge all people justly, bring help to the oppressed and afflicted, restrain the wicked, and protect the good, win the minds of all to themselves, so that they are ready and prepared for any kind of obedience. Eusebius relates a memorable example of this concerning Constantius, the father of Constantine the Great. A rumour had spread about him that he was mild, and that by sparing his subjects he was emptying his treasuries and had nothing stored away in them. So Diocletian, sending envoys to him, accused him of laziness and neglect of the commonwealth and reproached him with poverty. But Constantius, having ordered the envoys to wait, announced to all the peoples of his realm that he had need of money. Without delay, they eagerly and with glad hearts filled his treasuries with gold and silver, to the great astonishment of the envoys, who learned from this event how much more useful it is to bind a wealthy people to oneself by justice and fairness than to alienate the minds of all by unjust exactions. On the contrary, it is certain that those who disregarded the laws and did everything according to their own lust never reigned long or happily. But which of the kings of this age will you name who could, even in this respect, be compared with Christ? For though he is Lord of all, and by his own right could claim all goods for himself, yet he never extorted anything from anyone, and he has never used any man's house or property against his will. And now he did not wish to use even a donkey's colt for a few hours without the consent of its owners, who were unwilling. This both commends his kingdom and makes it pleasing to all good people, and it refutes the frenzies of those fanatics who, under the pretext of the Gospel, try to force a community of all goods even on those who are unwilling and resisting. Although these men can be refuted without difficulty by the custom and practice of the primitive Church (whose example they shamefully abuse) and by many other arguments, this one point ought to be enough for us against them: that Christ is known to have done this during the whole time he lived on earth. Now let the first part of this history serve us for this purpose: that we acknowledge Jesus Christ to be the truly wise, powerful, and most just King, and therefore that we worship him and place our confidence of salvation in him alone.
