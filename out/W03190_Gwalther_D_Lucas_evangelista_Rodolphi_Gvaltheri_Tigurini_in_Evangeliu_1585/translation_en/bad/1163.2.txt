[1] Lastly, the Lord enjoins obedience on his disciples, and requires a specimen or example of it by a special and temporary command. For he bids them not to leave the city until they are clothed with power from on high, that is, until they receive the Spirit promised to them. Just as God once tested obedience at the very beginning of things, in the case of our first parents, when he forbade them to eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, so now the same command is renewed in the restoration of the human race. For although he had long since destined the disciples for the office of teaching, and although he had instructed them for a long time in private, and had now opened their minds so that they might understand the Scriptures, yet he does not want them to claim for themselves the knowledge of the work to be done, nor to rush forth to teach, carried away by rash confidence in their own powers or in their own dignity; rather they are to await the hour of their calling or sending, when they are to be endowed with a fuller power of the Spirit, so that they may be equal to so great a task. And that this test of obedience was not superfluous, will easily be understood by anyone who considers more closely the fall of Adam and the corruption of the whole of nature that has come down to us from it. For just as we are by nature desirous of knowledge, and often, inflamed by excessive curiosity, want to know even those things that have nothing to do with us, so we often persuade ourselves that we are already more than sufficiently equipped for any task whatever, and we do not notice that those things which are chiefly necessary are still lacking to us. Indeed, if we consider the disciples, the Lord had certainly already bestowed much upon them; yet the hope of an earthly kingdom still clung to their minds, than which nothing more absurd, nothing more alien to their office, could be imagined. Moreover, they still lacked that unconquerable courage of spirit, and lacked the gift of tongues and of interpretation, without which it was impossible to preach the Gospel throughout the whole world. He rightly bids them therefore to wait, lest by attempting something out of season they betray their nakedness (as Adam once did) and expose themselves and the Gospel to the mockery of the whole world. All men of every place and rank ought to apply this to themselves, so that they do not undertake any arduous office without the lawful calling of God. In the Church, certainly, that kind of men is most pestilent who, by seeking the office of teaching, either throw the whole order of the Church into confusion, and are often the authors of schisms; which we experience today in the Anabaptists. In the commonwealth, however, those do the greatest harm who seize the reins of government before they are lawfully called to them, and think it enough that they were born of respectable station, and have persuaded themselves that they have joined to this some practical experience of affairs as well. Let all therefore remember that the safest course in carrying on affairs is to wait for God, who from of old, by the counsel of his eternal providence, has destined each one to his end, and who, whom he calls, he also equips with the necessary gifts, so that they may be able to satisfy their office, and who in time, together with all the elect, enter into the joy of their Lord Jesus Christ. To him be blessing, honour, glory and power for ever, Amen.
