[1] <i>Here, moreover, God's wonderful goodness, more than fatherly, shines forth. In regard to all the actions of our lives, he has imposed on us no laws that are hard to understand or to carry out for those who use sound judgement. He does not burden us with other people's opinions, but leaves each person to the will of his own mind. If each person examines this more carefully, he will soon see that what first seemed unjust and utterly intolerable is just and fair. Indeed, we will be inexcusable if we transgress the rights or laws of our own will. For if we quickly notice the faults of others, how much more easily shall we detect our own errors, if this lamp is applied to us? This saying ought therefore to be applied to every action of life, and to be inscribed in all places, public and private. Alexander Severus the Emperor saw this. Although he did not know the Christian religion, he understood that this law was contained in nature, and so he had it written on the walls of his palace and on other public buildings: WHAT YOU DO NOT WISH TO BE DONE TO YOU, DO NOT DO TO ANOTHER. Meanwhile, while men were being beaten with rods, he wished the same saying to be publicly proclaimed by a herald. From the same source he also drew his military laws, whose substance he prescribed to the tribunes of the soldiers in roughly these words: If you wish to be a tribune, or rather if you wish to live, restrain the hands of the soldiers. Let no one trample the crops; let no one carry off salt, oil or wood. Let no one snatch another's sheep. Let the soldier be content with his own rations. Let him have his plunder from the spoils of the enemy, not from the tears of the provincials. Let the ministers of the churches therefore meditate on this constantly. They ought to wish that sound doctrine be set before them, and that they not be led astray by the harmful traditions of men. Let them take care that they themselves be not authors of any error for anyone; let them preach the Word of God purely, and neglect no one's salvation. Let those who hold public office likewise repeat the same thing to themselves. If they were in the place of subjects, they would not wish to be burdened with taxes and excessive exactions, but would wish justice to be done in a good cause for them, justly and without any regard for persons. Let them therefore faithfully render the same duties to the people committed to their trust. Let merchants and craftsmen inscribe these words on their workshops. And since no one wishes to be deceived, or oppressed by unfair usury, or cheated in any other way, let them also abstain from frauds and from all those things that are connected with loss to their neighbour. Similarly, let them think carefully about being a servant, and weigh what they would wish done to themselves if they were compelled to serve others; and thus they will command their own people far more humanely and gently. Servants, in turn, while they demand faithfulness and fairness from others, should also faithfully render due obedience to their masters. Moreover, let this be the rule for spouses: that each should keep unblemished the faithfulness and love that he demands from his partner. Thus they will feel that there is no room for adulteries or other vices that are opposed to the laws of marriage. Likewise, those who regard some people as enemies, or think they have been wronged by others, should at once turn their minds back to the consideration of this saying; and soon they will see what it becomes them to do. This saying therefore contains everything that the philosophers once embraced in countless volumes, and it convicts of vanity and folly all those who complain that they do not know how to order their lives.</i>
