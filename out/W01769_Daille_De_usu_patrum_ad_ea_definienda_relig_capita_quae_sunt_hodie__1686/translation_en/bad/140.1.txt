[1] It is plainly not in their interest to preserve that reverence, at least in outward appearance, intact and covered. But while they attack so fiercely those poor fellows who compiled indexes of the works of the Fathers, they are, as the proverb says, whitewashing two walls with one bucket of lime. They do tear out the hated opinions, by treating these more recent writers so harshly, and they testify to the reverence for the ancients that their cause requires, by sparing the Fathers, the true authors of those opinions. Yet even this is not simply true, that they spare the Fathers, whose many monuments they have destroyed for more than one reason, as the examples we have mentioned, or shall soon mention, make clear. For were not those three hundred bishops of the Synod of Frankfurt Fathers, whose canons Binius suppressed in his edition of the Councils, because of the definition about the worship of images that the Nicene Council had condemned at the Second Council of Nicaea? Were not the bishops of the Synod of Paris under Louis the Pious Fathers, whose book these men try by every means to discredit and abolish, and which the Spanish censors expressly list among the prohibited books, calling it "a book falsely put out in the name of the Emperor Louis against sacred images"? Yet it is so far from being "falsely put out" that, as we shall say presently, it is acknowledged as a true and genuine product of the bishops gathered at Paris by Louis in the year 824 by the famous Jesuit Sirmond himself. The same Sirmond also holds those books of Charles to be genuine and written in the eighth century, which the Tridentine book-burners have placed in the list of forbidden and destroyable works. What shall I say of the Synod of Reims, whose acts were published six hundred and fifty years ago by the man who afterwards sat as Pope at Rome, and which those good descendants now dispute and delete from all their books? Certainly among the ancient Doctors of the Church that famous John Erigena, or Scotus (that is, as we would say today, the Irishman), also shines; his book on the Eucharist existed nearly two hundred years after the author's death, as we have already said. Nor can a reason be given why Rathramnus of Corbie should not also be counted among the Fathers, whose treatment of the same subject, disputed, we shall presently show how badly these men have received.

[3] Nor indeed was Huldaric, the earlier bishop of Augsburg of that name, one of the more recent writers; he was a contemporary (as it seems) of Rathramnus. Their censors simply strike out his letter to Nicholas I, written in the ninth century in defence of the marriage of priests, both in the Tridentine and in the Spanish indexes. † The most noble writer Sigebert of Gembloux must also be reckoned among the ancients; it is established that he flourished five hundred years ago, and, to go beyond that, at the very beginning of the twelfth century, just as the eleventh century was ending. Yet not even his works have been spared. I pass over the shameful marks with which the Spanish censors have disfigured his chronicle, a most praised work. † But these men, and before them the Tridentine men themselves as well, have placed in each of their indexes Sigebert's notable disputation on the papal tyranny over princes, first usurped by Hildebrand in his own time, as a work devoted to destruction, in these words: "Sigebert's book against Pope Gregory, and against the letter of Pope Paschal." About the same time was written the book "On preserving the unity of the Church," an apologetic work for the Emperor Henry IV against Hildebrand; whether we attribute it to Venerius of Vercelli, or to Walram of Naumburg, or finally to Conrad, bishop of Utrecht. Yet against this very book the Spaniards pronounced that grim sentence: "Let the whole Apology of Henry IV be cut away." Nor have they judged more gently the sacred letters of the same Emperor: "Let the name of the letter of the Emperor Henry IV be expunged," they say. All the less is it to be wondered at that they have acted in the same way against Honorius Augustodunensis, Dante, Petrarch, Theodoric of Niem, Ockham, and other writers of later times, although these are, for our century, almost ancient. Nor have they passed over a certain disputation between a cleric and a soldier on civil and ecclesiastical power, by an anonymous author, yet written before the year 1580. But our Romanists do not now even abstain from those things that had been consecrated by the public use of their own Church. For the Spanish Index, from a certain book whose title was "The order of baptizing together with the manner of visiting, according to the rite of the Holy Roman Church," orders these three passages that follow to be deleted: (1) "Do you believe that you come to glory not by your own merits, but by the power and merit of the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ?" (2) "Do you believe that our Lord Jesus Christ died for our salvation, and that no one can be saved by his own merits, or in any other way, except in the merit of his passion?" (3) "There is no need to despair or doubt of the salvation of one who with his heart believes the petitions set out above and confesses them with his mouth." These the Spanish censors, judging them alien to the doctrine of human merits before God, which is theirs, and wholly and entirely consonant with the opposed opinion of the Protestants, order to be expunged from that book, printed at Venice in the year 1575 by John Guarisco and partners. And the Roman Index had already condemned this unhappy book, "unless it be corrected." Yet that these things, which those men now delete, were also in common use earlier among their own forebears, even at Rome, both the book itself teaches, and Cardinal Hosius, a most abundant witness, who says he saw a "Roman Sacerdotal," in which those very things that they now delete (for he describes them) were found word for word; and finally the exhortation of Anselm of Canterbury to a dying brother, and other formularies in which wholly similar things are read. These, indeed, are the arts by which they have tried to extinguish and abolish whatever is opposed to the doctrine of the Romanists. If any book of this kind from the ancients, by some happy good fortune of its own genius, has survived to our time, overcoming all the injuries of times and men, not even so will it be permitted to enjoy or use it in these disputations about religion. For the Romanist theologians, as if by a conspiracy, usually rise up against such a book; they remove it from the world, if that is allowed, and if not, they slander it and defame it, and they move every stone to make its author infamous. Though they cannot achieve this, they at least either expunge the testimony itself or distort it by various frauds, and bring it about, so that it may not be of use. Thus Bellarmine (to illustrate the matter with a single example), seeing that the works of Mark the Hermit contained much about faith and about our justice before God, alien to the present decrees of Rome, defamed them, writing that he suspected they had been "either forged by heretics of our time or greatly corrupted," that is, by men alien to the communion of the Roman Pope. The book of Gelasius, Roman Pope, on the two natures of Christ against Eutyches, in which a notable passage against transubstantiation is found, exists. Since the Roman theologians could not deny that the book was ancient, they took refuge in contending that it was by another author than Gelasius of Rome, which, learned men, and as men who understand the great authority of their own Pontiff, they would have to recognise. First, therefore, Baronius, Bellarmine, Petronius and others, gathering sophisms and tricks from every side, fight that it is not by Gelasius, bishop of Rome, and then they transcribe the wretched little work, cast out from the true and genuine family of its parent, under a foreign and less noble name. Others affirm that it is the offspring of a Gennadius, others of some Gelasius of Caesarea, I know not which, and others finally of another Gelasius of Cyzicus; so that the testimony of an author of uncertain identity should carry no faith with men. In vain: for two authors of the highest standing, beyond all exception, Fulgentius of Ruspe and John, have long since asserted this very little book to be by Pope Gelasius.
