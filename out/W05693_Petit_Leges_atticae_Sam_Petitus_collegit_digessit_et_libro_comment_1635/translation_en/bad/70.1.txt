The Vulgate read <i>cærimonias etiam Sacerdotibus. nam à patribus acceptos,</i> etc. The old Aldine edition reads the same, except that it has <i>esse Sacerdotibus</i>. Gruter's manuscripts are closer, reading <i>cærimonias nosse Sacerdotibus. nam patribus</i> acceptos, etc. Marcus Tullius, in these words, sets out the law he was proposing: NO ONE SHALL HAVE GODS APART, NOR NEW ONES; BUT NOT FOREIGN ONES EITHER, UNLESS ADOPTED PUBLICLY; THEY SHALL WORSHIP THEM IN PRIVATE. On the matter of foreign gods, Tullius has himself raised the objection: Is it not lawful to worship foreign gods received from the priests or from the fathers? Cicero replies that it is lawful, provided they are publicly adopted, as this law prescribed, and not by private authority of fathers or priests. So this is the sense of Tullius's words, which lies hidden and escapes readers, because the mark of interrogation has fallen out. It must be restored to its place and set back in this form: <i>Suosque Deos, aut novos, aut alienigenas coli, confusionem habet religionum, & ignotas caerimonias. Non à Sacerdotibus, non à patribus acceptos Deos? Ita placet coli, si huic Legi paruerint ipsi.</i> But the Romans, no less than the Athenians, easily strayed into foreign religions, and on this matter the passage of Tertullian in the first book <i>Ad Nationes</i>, chapter 13, is remarkable: <i>Vos certe estis, qui etiam in laterculum septem dierum Solem recepistis, & ex diebus ipsorum praelegistis, quo die lauacrum subtrahatis, aut in vesperam differatis, aut otium & prandium curetis. Quod quidem facitis exorbitantes & ipsi à vestris ad alienas religiones: Iudaei enim festi, Sabbata, & cena pura, & Iudaici ritus lucernarum, & ieiunia cum azymis, & orationes litorales, quae utique aliena sunt à Diis vestris. Quare, ut ab excessu revertar, qui Solem, & diem eius nobis exprobratis, agnoscite vicinitatem: non longe à Saturno & Sabbatis vestris sumus.</i> I have set out the whole passage so that it can be corrected. First, the words <i>qui etiam in laterculum septem dierum Solem recepistis</i> are wholly corrupt. Although the passage concerns the observance of the week, which the Romans began to use shortly before this time, and whose day-names they took from the planets, nevertheless the words that immediately follow, <i>et ex diebus ipsorum praelegistis, etc.</i>, sufficiently show that [illegible, ~1 letters] <i>dierum</i>, to which [illegible, ~1 letters] <i>ipsorum</i> refers, is not sound. I therefore rewrite it as <i>qui etiam in laterculum septem Deorum Solem recepistis:</i> for the pagans reckoned the seven planets among their gods, and it was from them that the days of the week received their names.
