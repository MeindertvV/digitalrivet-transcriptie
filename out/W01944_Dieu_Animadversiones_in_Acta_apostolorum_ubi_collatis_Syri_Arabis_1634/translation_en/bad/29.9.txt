[1] VERSE 30. "Τὸ χ̅ σώρκα ἀναστήσει τ Χριστόν" [the fruit of your loins will raise up the Christ]. The Vulgate, the Syriac, the Arabic and the Ethiopic all omitted this whole clause. As for Beza's claim that the Arabic and the Syriac read "Ἐγὼ ἐκ καρπῆς τ ὀσφύος σου ἀναστήσω, καθίσαι ἐπὶ ἕ θρόνου σου" [I will raise up from the fruit of your loins, to sit upon your throne], that is, <i>I will raise up from the fruit of your loins one whom I will place on your throne</i>, I do not know where he got it from the Syriac. As for the Arabic, he followed Junius, who, having translated the Arabic text correctly as <i>I will make one from the fruit of your loins to sit upon your throne</i>, and having noted that the received reading "τὸ χ̅ σώρκα ἀναστήσει τ Χριστόν" smells of a gloss, added that perhaps the Arabic had read the text more completely in the way Beza mentioned above. Yet the words of the Arabic have not been rendered into Greek correctly. The Arabic reads "ὅτι ἐγὼ ἐκ καρπῆς τ ὀσφύος σου καθίσω ἐπὶ ἕ θρόνου σου" [because I will set from the fruit of your loins upon your throne], that is, <i>I will set (make to sit) from the fruit of your loins upon your throne</i>. The Syriac does the same. Both render word for word the words of Psalm 132:11, "מפרי בטנך אשית לכסא לך" [of the fruit of your body I will set upon your throne], except that the Syriac has the plural <i>fruits</i> where the Hebrew has the singular "fruit", and the Arabic has <i>loins</i> where the Hebrew has "body" (belly). I cannot see, however, why we should suspect a gloss in the received reading. The Vulgate, the Syriac, the Arabic and the Ethiopic evidently followed the Hebrew text, since they took the same view as Junius, but there appears to be no reason why that reading, which the Greek manuscripts exhibit and Chrysostom expressed, should be suspected even slightly. For since Luke does not simply report what was sworn to David, but what David, as a prophet, knew had been sworn to him, he ought not to have recited the bare words of the promise, but to explain how David, as a prophet, understood them. God had promised David that one of the fruit of his body would be set upon his throne. David, as a prophet, knew this meant nothing other than that God would raise up, as regards the flesh, from the fruit of his loins, Christ, to sit upon his throne. I do not translate "καθίσαι" as "whom he would place", supplying "ἐν", but as "that he might sit", supplying "ἐκ τῆς", which is most usual, and I take "καθίσαι" intransitively, which is the most common sense of this verb throughout the whole New Testament. Nor do I refer "ἀναστήσει" to the resurrection of Christ, but to his natural production, as also in 2 Sam. 7:12, "ἀναστήσω τὸ σπέρμα σου μετὰ σὲ, ὃς ἐξελεύσεται ἐκ τῆς κοιλίας σου" [I will raise up your seed after you, who shall come forth from your womb]. His resurrection is treated in the following verse, and I refer this verse of ours thus: David, understanding by the Prophetic Spirit that it had been promised to him in Psalm 132 and 2 Samuel 7 that Christ would be raised up from his seed, by the same prophetic foreknowledge spoke in Psalm 16 about his resurrection. Incidentally, for the benefit of the philosophers, I add that in the Regia and Cothenensis editions there is read "ܥܠ ܟܘܪܣܝܐ ܕܝܢ" [ʿal kursyāʾ dên], <i>upon your throne</i>. Correct this and read "ܥܠ ܟܘܪܣܝ ܕܝܢ" [ʿal kursî dên], as the Viennese edition has it, though it is rather obscure; both usage and grammar require it. For it is without the suffix, "ܟܘܪܣܝ" [kursî], <i>throne</i>.
