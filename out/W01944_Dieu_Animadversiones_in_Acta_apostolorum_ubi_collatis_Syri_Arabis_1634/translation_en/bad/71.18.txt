[1] VERS. 42. <i>And God turned and gave them up</i> [Ἔστρεψε δὲ ὁ Θεὸς, καὶ παρέδωκε] &c. Vulgate: <i>God turned and delivered them.</i> Erasmus: <i>turned himself.</i> Beza: <i>turned himself.</i> That is, he turned his favour into anger. The Syriac has <i>aphinu</i>, and the Arabic <i>and he returned.</i> This may have the same sense as the preceding clause, for <i>to return</i> is used of God in Hosea 5.15: <i>I will go and return to my place</i> [אֵלְכָה אָשׁוּבָה אֶל מְקוֹמִי], <i>I will go, I will return to my place.</i> But the sense could also be, <i>and he returned and delivered them</i>, that is, <i>and again he delivered them up.</i> The Hebrews can render this in no other way than <i>and he returned</i> [וַיָּשָׁב]. The meaning is this: just as they had <i>turned</i> [ἐστράφησαν] back in their hearts to Egyptian idolatry, so God <i>turned</i> [ἔστρεψε], that is, <i>having turned, he delivered them</i> [στρέψας παρέδωκε] again, so that they should serve the idols which they had served earlier in Egypt. VERS. 43. <i>And you took up the tabernacle of Moloch, which you made</i> [Καὶ ἀνελάβετε τὸν σκηνὴν τοῦ Μολὸχ, ὃ ἐποιήσατε]. The passage of Amos 5, verse 26, from which St Stephen drew these words, reads as follows: <i>You have borne Sikkuth your king and Kiyyun your images</i> [הַנְּשָׂאתֶם אֶת סִכּוּת מַלְכְּכֶם וְאֶת כִּיּוּן צַלְמֵיכֶם]. Here by <i>your king</i> [מַלְכְּכֶם] is not meant an earthly king, since the Israelites did not yet have one, but a heavenly one, namely the Sun, whom the Orientals called now <i>Baal</i>, or in Syriac <i>Beel, Bel</i>, that is, <i>Lord</i>, and now <i>king</i> [מֶלֶךְ], <i>the king</i>, otherwise <i>malk</i> [מלך], from which the Syriac and Arabic here have <i>malkam</i> [מלכם] for the Greek <i>Moloch</i> [μόλοχ]. Whoever wishes more on all these matters should consult the very accurate treatise on the Syrian gods by the most learned John Selden, where he deals with Moloch and shows that <i>malkam</i> is rightly rendered <i>Moloch</i> [μόλοχ], and that <i>sikkuth</i> [סכות] is rightly rendered <i>tabernacle</i> [σκηνὴν] by Luke, because idolaters in the most ancient rite used to carry their gods about in tents and booths. But what <i>Rephan</i> [Ῥεμφὰν] is, so far as I know, no one has yet explained satisfactorily. All admit that it stands for the Hebrew <i>Kiyyun</i> [פְּרָ]. Ibn Ezra, however, notes that it is made up of Arabic and Persian, and that the star of Saturn is called <i>Shabtai</i> [שבתאי], which is quite certain. For in a certain Persian-Turkish etymological dictionary, which the distinguished Jacob Golius, the light of our Academy, brought from the East, entitled <i>Danistan</i>, in the first chapter, where the names of the stars are listed, we have seen <i>Kaywan</i>, <i>Shabtai</i> [کیوان, שבתאי] recorded for <i>Saturn</i>. And in an Arabic lexicon called the <i>Qamus</i>, <i>Kaywan</i>, <i>Shabtai</i> is explained as <i>mardun mutanazzi'un</i>, <i>a stern and austere man</i>, properly one who is forbidden, to whom access is as it were forbidden on account of his moroseness and difficult character. Nor among the astrologers is any of the planets more harsh than Saturn. But just as there was always great confusion of the gods among the Gentiles, so Saturn signified not only the star I have mentioned, but also Bel or Jupiter, and was therefore regarded as the same as Moloch, and for that reason was sacrificed to with human victims, as Selden shows at length in his chapters on Moloch and Bel. Just as our Prophet rightly joined <i>Saturn</i> and <i>king</i> [שבת & מלך], so too... For whether you say Saturn was the Sun, or time, or both together, the ancients surely attributed to him the first and highest rule and kingdom, on which all things depend, and who is the author of all generation and corruption. In Orpheus Saturn is called <i>father of gods and men</i> [Θεῶν πατὴρ ἡδὲ ἀνδρῶν]. And the Platonists indeed called that divine mind, which embraces the rest, now Jupiter, now Venus, now Saturn: Jupiter, insofar as it is understood to govern the ethereal region; Venus, insofar as, descending into lower bodies, it stirs them to generation; but Saturn, insofar as the power of the divine mind, coming from heaven, diffuses itself into all things and holds together the whole light and providence of all things, which pertains to order and life and the changes of all fortunes.
