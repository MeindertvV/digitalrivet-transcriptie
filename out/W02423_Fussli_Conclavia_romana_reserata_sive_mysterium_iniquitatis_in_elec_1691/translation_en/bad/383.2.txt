[1] If, however, the adoration has been secured from the number of electors believed sufficient, the scrutiny is nonetheless still carried out. But always either the one adored or the adorers call out that it is to be done without prejudice to the adoration already made, and this protest is sometimes also made in writing, through a notary. And indeed, in the case of Sixtus V, the author of his Life first interprets it as an outward act of ambition: when the Dean, after the adoration had been made, ordered everyone to take their seats for the scrutiny, he said in the ear of his chief promoter, Cardinal S. Sisto: <i>Press the point that the scrutiny be made without prejudice to the adoration.</i> That is: <i>Insist that the scrutiny be made without prejudice to me.</i> And obeying his instruction, he cried out twice or three times, together with the other supporter, Cardinal Alessandrino: <i>Without prejudice to the adoration.</i> That is: <i>Without prejudice to the adoration.</i> And when the elect then began <i>to lift away those mists of pretence that for more than fifteen years had kept hidden the great ambition that reigned in his heart</i> (tollere nebulas illas simulationum, quae tenebant occultam ultra 15 annos ingentem ambitionem, quae dominabatur in corde ejus), and he threw away the staff that had been his support until then, stood upright, and seemed taller on his feet, and spat out with such vehemence as a young man of thirty could hardly have shown, while the Cardinals looked at one another in amazement, the Dean said in a loud voice, seeing this transformation in Montalto and the signs of repentance noted in others: <i>Wait, there is an error in the votes, the scrutiny is not good.</i> That is: <i>Wait, there is an error in the votes, the scrutiny is not good.</i> <i>It is good,</i> he replied, and then he intoned, in a voice so resonant and high, the <i>Te Deum laudamus</i> that was customarily sung after an election, that the echo filled the whole Sala, coming from the man who before had uttered scarcely a word except after coughing two or three times. Following this example, that wretched man of S. Severina also wished the scrutiny to be made without prejudice to the adoration: <i>Gentlemen,</i> he said to those wanting to scrutinise, <i>I protest that everything be done without prejudice to me.</i> That is: <i>Gentlemen, I protest that everything be done without prejudice to me.</i> And the Dean replied that all agreed, though meanwhile everything ought to be done out of love and pardon ought to be given: <i>all agree, yet meanwhile everything must be done out of love, and he must be forgiven.</i> To which the other answered: <i>As if I did not want to pardon him! Rather I want to be called Clement, so as to show by name and by deeds that they will find me the opposite of what they have imagined;</i> That is: <i>As if I would not wish to pardon them! Rather I want to be called Clement, to show by name and deeds that I will be other than they have imagined.</i> But Clement, in vain as it turned out, soon wished to be called by the name of the object of another Clemency, the Clement who came forth from that Conclave.
