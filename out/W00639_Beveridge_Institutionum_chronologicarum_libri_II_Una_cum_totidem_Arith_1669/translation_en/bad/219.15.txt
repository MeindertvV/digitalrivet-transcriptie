[1] In this table the vulgar forms are placed on the left, under the various figures by which the Orientals give them, as set out above. The ancient forms, which our forebears used, occupy the middle place. I have seen them used in ancient Western manuscripts, and they are described here with the same figures. Last come the vulgar forms in use today. From this comparison it is also plain how the vulgar forms are derived from the Indian ones. Unity is drawn with the same figure in all of them. The vulgar 2 and 3 are the Indian figures themselves, only placed differently for speed of writing: thus 2 3, with the character <i>ɥ</i> not diminished at all, and <i>ɯ</i> docked only by part of its perpendicular line. The character <i>ɔ</i>, reversed and mutilated by a lengthened line, becomes <i>u</i>; in its place the ancients used the figure <i>ℵ</i>, and we use 4, with almost no difference admitted, except that the head of our figure is turned into a scalene. In the rest, apart from 9 and the cipher, no major difference appears. For although nearly the same characters are retained, their values have been changed, one taking the place of another. The fifth character <i>ϑ</i> is moved from the fifth to the eighth place, and our forebears used it to denote eight; with little alteration <i>ϑ</i> is shaped into our 8. And just as the fifth is advanced to the eighth place, so the eighth, <i>ʌ</i> or <i>†</i>, is pushed back into the seventh place. The former is used by the ancients, the latter by us, to denote the number seven. But since the ancients had advanced the fifth figure into the eighth place, it was necessary that another figure should fill the place of the fifth. For this purpose the sixth figure <i>ɯ</i> is assigned, by which the ancients signified the number five. At last, becoming more corrupted, it is changed into <i>ხ</i>, and finally into 5. And now no figure remains from which 6 could be derived, apart from the seventh <i>v</i>; how this could have been formed from it is entirely unclear to me. The last two characters, q and 0, were brought here from the East almost untouched by any change, except that our forebears had pierced the cipher ɵ with a line, which we have pulled out so that its original form is restored. Indeed a small circle, whether pierced by a line or empty of one, always signified the cipher to us, and never a point. This is because we trace these characters to the Arabs, who, if not alone, at least more often than the other Orientals, signified the cipher by a circle and the number five universally by its letter <i>ɡ</i>.
