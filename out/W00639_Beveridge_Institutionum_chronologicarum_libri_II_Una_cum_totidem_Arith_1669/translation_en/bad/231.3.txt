[1] I place the Greek characters after the Hebrew ones, because just as the Greek letters themselves were derived from the Hebrew, so was their numerical value. This is sufficiently shown by the fact that, so that all numbers can be expressed by Greek letters as well as Hebrew ones, three numerical signs, or <i>stigma</i> [Ϛίγμα], <i>episemon vau</i> [Ἐπίσημον βαῦ], <i>koppa</i> [κόππα], and <i>san</i> [Σὰν] ΙΓ, are inserted into the numerical Greek alphabet. The first is the <i>episemon vau</i> [Ἐπίσημον βαῦ], or <i>episemon</i> that comes from the numbers [ἐπίσημον τὸ ἐξ ἀριθμῶν], which is drawn in a double form, ϛ and F. Although there are some who call the first form ϛ the <i>episemon</i> that comes from the numbers [ἐπίσημον τὸ ἐξ ἀριθμῶ], and the second F the <i>episemon vau</i> [ἐπίσημον βαῦ], both may not unfairly be called <i>episemon vau</i> [ἐπίσημον βαῦ], since both signs are the <i>episema</i> [ἐπίσημα], or marks, of the Hebrew letter <i>Vau</i>. Indeed the Greeks, like the Phoenicians and Hebrews, express numbers by the letters of the alphabet. Hence <i>Alpha</i>, <i>Beta</i>, <i>Gamma</i>, <i>Delta</i>, <i>Epsilon</i> have the same numerical value as <i>Aleph</i>, <i>Beth</i>, <i>Gimel</i>, <i>Daleth</i>, <i>He</i>, namely 1, 2, 3, 4, 5; and so too <i>Zeta</i>, <i>Eta</i>, <i>Theta</i>, <i>Iota</i> have the same value as <i>Zain</i>, <i>Heth</i>, <i>Teth</i>, <i>Jod</i>, namely 7, 8, 9, 10; and so on. But in the Greek alphabet there is no letter to express the number six, which is represented by the Hebrew letter <i>Vau</i>. Hence the Greeks, after <i>Epsilon</i>, the fifth letter, corresponding to the Hebrew ⌐, inserted another figure not belonging to the letters, which they called the <i>episemon</i>, or mark of the letter <i>vau</i> [βαῦ], or <i>Vau</i>, as a substitute for the Hebrew <i>Vau</i> and having the same numerical value. Further, as <i>episemon vau</i> corresponds to the Hebrew letter <i>Vau</i>, so <i>koppa</i> corresponds to the letter <i>Koph</i>, and <i>san</i> [Σαν] to <i>Tsade</i>. But it happened that these two <i>episema</i> were transposed in the Greek alphabet: for <i>koppa</i> took the place of <i>Tsade</i>, and <i>san</i> the place of <i>Koph</i>. Indeed each of them has the same numerical value as <i>Tsade</i>: the <i>episemon koppa</i> is the same as the initial ϥ or <i>Tsade</i>, and the <i>episemon san</i> ΙΓ is the same as the final ϥ. Therefore the <i>episemon koppa</i>, or <i>koph</i>, marked thus G or ϥ, is the <i>episemon</i> of ninety [ἐπίσημον ἐκκενόντα], that is, having the value 90; but the <i>episemon santa</i> [Σάντα], marked thus ͵, is the <i>episemon</i> of nine hundred [ἐπίσημον ἐννακοσίων], having the value 900. Concerning these three Greek numerical signs there is a notable passage in Bede, in his book on Indigitation, which is certainly worth transferring here: <i>For the Greeks express all numbers by all their letters; but when their whole alphabetic character has been extended to the figures of numbers, they depict the three numbers that are beyond these with their own marks, which are not from the alphabet.</i> The first is ϛ, which is called Episimon, and is the mark of the number ϜΙ. The second is Ϙ, which is called Koph, and has the value of ϞC. The third is ϡ, which is called Enneacosia, because it has the value DCCCC. You see here the ancient form and value of these three signs. As for their shape, it is quite clear that these numerical <i>stigmata</i> [ςτίχμα] were, no less than the Greek letters, derived from the ancient Hebrew or Samaritan: for ϛ is formed from the letter ϗ, which when inverted becomes very like ϗ. Koppa Ϙ is formed from ϙ or P, for ϙ inverted becomes q, and P becomes ϙ, a figure which comes closer to Ϙ. San ϡ, however, is derived from ϡ, which corresponds to the Hebrew letter Tsade. Others, indeed, want <i>San pi</i> [Σαν πι] to be compounded of an inverted <i>San</i>, or Sigma, and <i>pi</i> [πι]; for in ancient times Sigma was drawn thus C, so when inverted it becomes Ↄ, and if you write ϝ inside it, it will be Ϥ.
