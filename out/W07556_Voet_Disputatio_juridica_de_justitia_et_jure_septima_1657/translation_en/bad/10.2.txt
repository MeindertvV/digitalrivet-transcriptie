Their arguments show: 1. Princes and others who hold rights of superiority may rightly forbid subjects to hunt, both on their own property, which they hold by private right, and in public woods and forests. Just as any private owner may by right forbid another to enter his land to hunt, fowl, or take bees and honey, as stated above, so public forests, seas, seashores and other public places have, by right of superiority, become as it were the private estates of princes: l. litora 3 ff. ne quid in loc. publ.; l. deprecatio 9 ff. ad L. Rhod. de jactu. 2. Princes and others who hold rights of superiority may forbid hunting even on the private lands of subjects, on account of the public interest: lest farmers, craftsmen and men of that kind, lured by the pleasure of hunting, abandon agriculture and trades; lest those given to hunting grow accustomed to robbery; and lest the species of animals be laid waste through excessive hunting and similar causes. 3. Princes and others who hold rights of superiority may, on account of the public interest, prescribe a set mode of hunting even among those subjects who possess the right to hunt, whenever public utility requires it. Joh. Faber and others, in their dissertation on §. feræ Inst. de rer. div., answer the arguments of these opponents; so do Fach., Zoan. (loc. cit.) and Eber. Speckhan qq. J. cent. 1 qu. 97. <i>Thus Frederick the Emperor prescribed a set mode of hunting for farmers, in c.1 §. si quis rusticus vers. nemo retia de pace tenenda in usf. Feud. Thus the civil law forbids, on frequented roads, the digging of pits and the setting of snares to catch wild beasts without any prior warning: l. qui fovéas 28 ff. ad L. Aquil. Thus the same civil law forbids fishermen to light fires on the seashore at night, lest sailors, supposing they are making for harbour, be deceived: l. ne piscatores ff. de incend. ruin. & naufr. Thus Saxon law forbids all hunting when the crops are standing, as the article on this point lays down, Sandrecht lib. 2 art. 61. Thus Saxon law allows a wild beast that runs into another's forest to be pursued with dogs only if the hunting horn is not sounded, Landrecht lib. 2 art. 61. Thus the same Saxon law forbids entering another's field to hunt, not only when the owner forbids it, see Weichbild under the title "one shall not hunt on other men's villages," and gloss on Weichbild art. 122, but in general, even if they have acquired a right of hunting on another's land by prescription, under penalty of one hundred florins, in the provincial ordinance "on hunting, enclosing and catching chickens." Thus our most illustrious Saxon dukes, at certain times (namely those in which people are chiefly given to generation and propagation), forbade hunting. Thus most just and moderate princes and others exercising jurisdiction have altogether taken away the hunting rights of some on their own estates, and have begun to claim those rights for themselves; which Geil. 2 obs. 68 n.10 does not think wholly unjust, provided that those exercising the hunting rights take care that wild animals do not graze on private fields, and, if it happens otherwise, make good the damage.</i> Here the interpreters dispute the following: whether the right of hunting and fowling in another's forest, and the power to forbid it to others, belongs to those who hold the hunting rights as a real servitude attached to the estate, as a personal servitude perpetually attached to jurisdiction as if it were proper to it, or by the name of a royal right? Zasius in sing. resp. lib. 1 c.11; Geill. 2 obs. 66; Mynsin[?] in Inst. ad §. inter rusticorum n.6 and 7, hold that it is a real servitude, an opinion which he says is received by the universal custom of Germany. Dn. Hackelm. qq. illustr. disp. 7 th.19 memb.1; Zoan. in rep. l.2 C. de pact. inter empt. & vendit.; Hartm. Hartmanni lib. 2 pr. obs. tit. 23 c.3. They call it a jurisdictional servitude, and Dn. Hackelm. (loc. cit.) thinks this view agrees with the laws; so Heig. 1 quaest. 15 n.44. Andreas Knichen, de Privil. Elect. & Ducum Saxoniae, and Pruckm. in tract. de Regalib. refer the rights of hunting to the royal rights, to which Vulteius replies, 1 Feud. c.5 n.7, and Niol[?], disp. feud. 11 th.3. We hold that the rights of hunting, according to their different aspects, are real servitudes (cf. l. quaesitum 12 §. si in agro 12; l. fundo 21 ff. de instit. vel inst. leg.), jurisdictional servitudes (cf. l. 28 ff. de servit.), and royal rights (see the fundamental c.unic. quae sunt regalia in verbo piscationum), and hence that today the Electors, princes and other estates of the Empire are enfeoffed with the rights of hunting. The following question also arises: may those who hold the rights of hunting rightly punish with death those who take wild beasts against their prohibition? By the older Saxon law no one may be punished capitally or corporally for hunting against the prohibition of princes, Landrecht lib. 1 art. 61; and we determine that this too must be observed today, with Wesemb.[?] in parat. ff. de acquir. rer. dom. n.7; Eberhard Speckhan[?] cent. 1 quaest. 98; Tiraquello de nobil. c.3.7 n.150; Marant de ord. jud. p.4 distl. n.45 and distl. 3 n.17; Sebast. Medic. in tract. de venat. piscat. & aucup. quaest. §. n.7; Goden. Consil. 70, who there adduce very elegant and altogether firm reasons. So much for hunting properly so called, and for fowling. Of this, the kind of hunting called fishing has its own different rules. As regards waters, one may fish freely in the sea, a river, a lake or a pond that is public, as the law of nations has it: d. §. feræ and §. apium Inst. de rer. div.; d. §. item feras. <i>By the most recent civil law, however, those invested with royal rights may today forbid fishing in public rivers by right of superiority: arg. c.unic. quae sint regalia in usib. feudor.; l.1 §. permittitur 41, junct. §. seq. ff. De aqua quotid. & aestiv.; l. sin. C. de aquae ductu.</i> Cf. Cæpoll. in tract. de servit. rustic. praed. rubr. de piscat.; Rosenth. in Synopsi juris feudalis c.5 quaest. 26. As to private waters, in a lake, pond or another's watercourse, whoever fishes there, even if no prohibition has been issued, may at once be sued on account of the land itself, by an action for injury or even for theft, and fish taken secretly may be extorted from him by the owner: l.13 in sin. ff. de injur.; d. §. item feras; l.3 §. quicquid autem ff. de acquir. rer. dom.; l. funes 15 ff. de act. empt.; l. venat. ff. de usur. Cf. Cujacius 4 Obs. 2; Duar. ad l. possideri §. item feras ff. de acq. poss., by that same text, because fish are, as it were, the fruits of the water, and stand in the same position as apples and other fruits, which may not be plucked from another's land without the crime of theft: t.t. de arbor. furt.; Cæl. If, however, fish from a pond placed on higher private ground are carried away into a public river by the bursting of the dykes, then anyone may take them freely, or he whose the royal rights are may occupy them: arg. d. §. quicquid autem. <i>But whether private persons, by long custom, can make a public river private, and whether, on the ground that they have been accustomed for many years to fish alone in some side-channel of a public river, they may exclude others from fishing by pretext of long custom, is rightly asked.
