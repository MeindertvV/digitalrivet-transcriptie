[1] These things are invented by the Adversaries [illegible] to frighten children away from our religion. We therefore answer these frenzied and slanderous consequences. First, we do not say that good works are <i>simply</i> sins, but only <i>per accidens</i>. Yet we acknowledge that they are true virtues, both as to <i>the substance of the action</i> and (in the regenerate) as to <i>a certain manner of acting</i>. But because of the vicious corruption of the old man, which rebels although it does not <i>rule</i>, they fall short of the required <i>degree of integrity</i>. These defects are plainly not only <i>beside</i> but also <i>against</i> the perfection of the divine law, whose letter <i>kills</i>. Yet these defects and imperfections can coexist with the grace of the Gospel, since they have been atoned for and blotted out by the merit of Christ, by whose blood they are expiated. They must, however, be seasoned by continual contrition of heart, without which all holiness is tainted, stinks, and <i>breeds a worm in the conscience</i>. With these things weighed, it will not be difficult to judge how good works, which come from the breath of the Holy Spirit and [illegible], are <i>true, good and praiseworthy</i>, and yet are <i>polluted</i> by our dregs. To prove this, the Jesuit {d}Vasquez hastens forward, a witness beyond all exception, who has given such testimony about the Protestants: <i>They say, he says, that the works by which eternal life is promised are indeed good in themselves, but that on account of a defect they certainly have something of the character of fault, yet are not <i>evil in their kind</i>; such are the furor ... </i>, and so on. Thus he. Indeed we do not say that imperfect works are distinguished from perfect and absolutely good works as pure wine from diluted, or as an apple wholly free of blemish from a blighted one. And the man who spoke to Christ in the Evangelist may serve as an example for us: <i>Lord, I believe; help my unbelief.</i> That word <i>I believe</i> shows true faith, but <i>help my unbelief</i> reveals that it was imperfect. It is objected that the righteous in Scripture would not have said that they had done God's commandments unless they had done them perfectly. {◦}Augustine satisfied this objection many centuries ago: <i>But I would put it this way</i>, he says, <i>that they did God's commandments in such a way that we remember the commandments themselves reach the morning, in which we daily truly say the promise of the holy sons: <i>THY WILL BE DONE</i>, and <i>FORGIVE US OUR DEBTS.</i> 2. We answer: since good works are good <i>in themselves</i> and are not evil except <i>per accidens</i>, we teach that they are to be done. For one who is going to teach a child to form letters does not forbid him to write because he does not yet write perfectly; nor is anyone so mad as to forbid a driver to urge on a donkey because it limps. Finally (as to the manner), God also praises good works and, although they are imperfect, approves them: not, however, by a debt of right, as in the <i>covenant</i> with the Jews, but by undeserved grace, as a <i>merciful Father</i>, as was said earlier from the Fathers.
