[1] For several centuries before Augustine, Origen promulgated our doctrine: <i>I can hardly persuade myself, he says, that there is any work which demands a reward from God as a debt.</i> Chrysostom agrees with him: <i>Eternal life is not something owed to us, or a wage.</i> (Above, chap. 8.) Basil, along with the other holy Fathers, follows the same path: <i>Eternal life is given not as something owed, or a wage, but as a gift.</i> This is surely the same Basil who said (above, chap. 8) <i>that eternal rest is not rendered according to what is owed.</i> Bede also stands on our side, on those words of Christ, <i>WHEN WE HAVE DONE WHAT WE OUGHT TO DO, WE HAVE DONE</i>: "Indeed, he says, we ought to, because HE CAME NOT TO BE MINISTERED UNTO, BUT TO MINISTER. <i>He has made us his debtors, lest we trust in our works, but always fear his judgement, so that we may say with the Prophet, WHAT SHALL WE RENDER TO THE LORD FOR ALL HIS BENEFITS,</i> etc." In this choir of holy Fathers, another sings sweetly in the same tune: <i>For human merits are not of such a kind that eternal life is owed to them by right, or that God would do any wrong, unless he gave it freely; for all merits are gifts of God, and man is more a debtor to God on account of them than God is to man.</i> Elias, the commentator on Gregory Nazianzen, learned this, so to speak, as the native language of the Fathers: <i>We ought to understand by mercy that reward which God pays back to us; for we, as servants, owe virtue, so that we may pay and offer all the best things, which are pleasing to God, as a kind of debt; since we have nothing which we have not received from him. But God, as Lord, shows mercy, and gives to us rather than repays us.</i>
