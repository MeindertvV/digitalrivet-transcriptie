[1] Another practice very common among the old English was to turn the letter C into an aspirate. Hence cornu, a horne; calamus, halme or haulme; cannabis, hempe; calx, a heele; capulus, a haft; caput, in Saxon heafod, now head. And, unless I am mistaken, cytisus was once hather, which I remember reading still survives in that sense among the Scots today. Later English applied the word to <i>Ericam</i>. Hence also the German hemel from cœlum, and our own hundred, I think, from <i>centum</i>, and house (which was once hafe and os) from <i>casa</i>. But more on this last word later. So also calidus became hott, the letter l being dropped, as happens often. There was therefore no reason for Julius Scaliger to reproach the Germans for reversing the natural order of things by substituting <i>calidum</i> or <i>caldum</i> for frigidus, as if they had fashioned their own kalt from the Latin <i>calidus</i>.
