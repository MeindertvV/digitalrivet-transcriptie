[1] I was most delighted, learned Sir, and always reverend to me, my Father, by everything you wrote back to me in your last letter, and especially by that most careful calculation by which you show that the year of the Hegira 920 agreed correctly with the year 1542 of our era. I hold all that you assert to be most certain. Nevertheless, what I warned you about, concerning those three examples you discussed, is also most true. I think this discrepancy arises because, as you well know, in human affairs it very often happens that one thing is found by theoretical speculation and another actually comes about in practice. In this matter especially it is no wonder, since those deficiencies or excesses of hours and minutes, which you noted so well, are disregarded in civil use and must necessarily be disregarded, so that the civil beginning of the year coincides with the beginning of the day on which it is set. But since the true astronomical beginning of the year differs by some hours and minutes from the civil beginning, which almost always either precedes or follows it, this difference, when neglected once and again, later causes us to find, over the long course of years, those large discrepancies which we have before us in our calculation. As for what you say about the eleventh day of <i>Aprilis</i> of the Christian year 1586, which my astrologer matched with the 22nd of the later Rabi of the Hegira year 994, do not be surprised that it seems to you that the 22nd of the later Rabi of that year coincides with the ninth or tenth of <i>Aprilis</i> in our reckoning. The Arabs, like the Persians and the Turks, fix their new moons not at the new moon, that is, at the conjunction of the luminaries, but at the sighting of the moon. Now since the moon does not begin to be seen until late, after sunset, and for them the night is always assigned not to the preceding day but to the following one, it follows that if, for example, the moon begins to be seen late on Tuesday, the following day, Wednesday, is counted as the first day of the month. Hence it comes about that the new moons fall two, and nearly always three, days after the new moons proper, and they do not advance at an equal pace. Some, like the Eastern Turks (that is, the inhabitants of the region of <i>Turkistan</i>, who are Tartars), celebrate them sooner, others, like the Persians, later. This clearly appears throughout all the ephemerides of those peoples. I have several ephemerides, both Persian and Turkish, as I have told you before. They are usually compiled for one year only, as is the custom in the East, since printing them for several years at once would not be easy for them, for lack of printing presses. Among these I value one above all, most carefully worked out by <i>Nulla Zeineddin Larita</i>, a mathematician and astronomer, the most distinguished among the Persians and my very dear friend. Indeed, even in our <i>Europa</i> he would have very few equals among the experts in this art, if he were not lacking in the books recently published among us, the instruments lately discovered, and finally the countless observations made by very learned men in these later centuries, all of which are unknown in Persia. He admitted to me that the more recent astronomical tables which he now uses, drawn up by <i>Olegh Chan</i>, prince of <i>Samarcand</i>, and by that great <i>Teimur Lenk, that is, Timur the Lame</i>, whom we call <i>Tamerlane</i> by a corrupted word, the son's son, are now too old and do not agree well enough with these times. Therefore the motions of some planets need correction in his tables, and very many other things are wanting. For this reason he asked me to send him some ephemerides and other books from our part of the world. In order that he might understand them, he wanted diligently to learn from me all the Arabic numerals, our astronomical characters, and also the letters of the alphabet and the manner of reading them. And if the ephemerides of <i>Magini</i> which I sent him have reached his hands, I do not doubt that he has perceived from them the greatest pleasure and usefulness. From this man, I say, with whom I was most familiarly acquainted through daily companionship for some months in the city of <i>Lar</i>, his homeland, I learned many things most worth knowing. I have ephemerides of one year, calculated for the meridian of the same city of <i>Lar</i>. But since he, being intent on greater matters, disdained to profess himself the author of these ephemerides, they are circulated under the name of <i>Mulla Enaiet Laritæ</i>, his full brother, who also himself practised astronomy. I received these, most carefully copied from the true author, as a gift from <i>Mulla Zeineddin</i> himself, in the city of <i>Lar</i>, as soon as they were published, for the same year with which they began. I have often been seized by a wonderful desire to have them in Latin, for I think they would be of the greatest use for chronology, because of the joining of different eras and the varied reckoning of months among different peoples, which is set before the eyes there. And, together with the aspects of the planets accurately placed in their certain positions, I do not doubt they would bring no small help to chronologists in examining dates. But several things have deterred me from this labour. First, because I think it will not easily happen at Rome that the superiors would grant the printing and publication of such things, and I would think it most difficult to have them printed elsewhere in my absence without countless errors, since everything is written in abbreviated script and is not easy to grasp unless one has long been versed in it. Moreover, the ordinary characters, although Persian and Arabic, might perhaps not suffice for printing the ephemerides, because of certain abbreviated forms, of which many abbreviations of letters are needed. Besides, I have no amanuensis to copy the Persian for me, and I myself am not equal to the labour, nor have I the patience to tire myself out with copying and waste my time. Moreover, since I keep no copy and the only one I have is the original, it would undoubtedly be very troublesome to send it elsewhere to be printed, with the danger of losing it on the journey. Nevertheless, perhaps I will overcome these difficulties and send you the whole ephemeris, in both scripts, Latin and Persian, if it can be printed in a clearly legible form [ἀμφιγνῶσαν]. Meanwhile, take from it what I send separately on another sheet chosen for this letter. And please let me know whether, at Paris, where Arabic type is certainly to be found, Persian may also be printed. It is indeed to be printed with the same Arabic characters, but beyond those which the Arabs use, three others are needed, which are not familiar to Arabs. These are not different in shape but have only a double value, distinguished from one another by certain small critical dots alone. For besides the Arabic letters ب B, ج E and ژ Z, which are also in use among the Persians and are pronounced by the Arabs in the same way, they use these same letters, when writing Persian and reading it with a certain peculiar Persian pronunciation, with the addition of critical dots giving them a different value, namely [illegible, about one letter] P, ج C (Italian) and [illegible, about one letter] (Spanish). It is therefore necessary, if we are to print Persian, to have these three characters in a double form, that is, marked with a single dot and the same three marked with three dots. While turning over my papers by chance, I found a sheet on which Father Thomas à Novaria copied some very ancient letters, either Hebrew or Egyptian or Arabic, which are found carved on many rocks in the desert of Mount <i>Sinai</i>, where, as is believed, the Hebrews wandered for so long through those places. There are several inscriptions, as you will see. Of these I saw only the first, at the foot of Mount <i>Horeb</i>; the rest I did not notice, being careless, while I was making the journey without knowing about this matter. But Father Novaria, passing that way more carefully, drew copies of these with the most exact diligence from among the many he saw. Thinking the matter one from which you could easily derive pleasure, I send you a copy on the sheet which you will receive together with this letter. I showed it to certain Hebrews here. Some of its letters seemed similar to the Hebrew letters in use today, others to the Samaritan, and others again agreed with neither. Yet no one could make out any sense from them. Whatever it is, you will perhaps understand it better. As for chronology, I ask you to look into the chronological work <i>cum Ubbonis Emni Groningæ</i> printed, which I myself have read, and which certainly did not displease me in all respects. But especially I found the era and the years of the Mahometans, and their joining with ours, more correctly arranged by this author than by others whom I have seen. He was led astray only in the genealogy of the more recent kings of Persia, being somewhat deceived by others. I corrected this in my little book on the king of the Persians. Please tell me, I beg you, what you think of this author. But come, I do not wish to weary you further. Only love me, and if I can do anything for your sake, do not cease to command me; I entreat you again and again.
