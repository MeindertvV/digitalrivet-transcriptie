[1] What is the point of heaping up more examples? Our opponents go so far that, by their own view, without some kind of inward illumination, the divinity of Scripture cannot even be rightly seen and recognised. Episcopius laughs at this, as we noted earlier. Thus Cloppenburg: <i>Faith by which we believe the Scriptures (which the Papists choose to call the authority of the Scriptures as far as we are concerned) is that conviction of mind and conscience by which the authority of Scripture is recognised, binding us to obedience of intellect and will. That faith does not exist without an inward illumination of the intellect</i>. He confirms this from passages of Scripture and adds: <i>These texts speak indeed of the regenerate and their saving faith, but by a certain analogy they also confirm the illumination of the unregenerate to that faith by which the Scriptures are believed.</i> Zanchius says: <i>Spiritual things cannot be perceived and truly known except by a supernatural light, and to give that light belongs only to God, who is above nature. It is therefore not the business of any man, nor even of the whole Church, but of God alone, to bring it about that someone certainly recognises that this Scripture of which we speak is God-breathed [θεόπνευστον]. Nor is it the work of Holy Scripture by itself to bring this about; the Holy Spirit must so illuminate the mind of the man reading or hearing Scripture that he sees and feels that God speaks in the Scriptures, not man.</i> Therefore, although Scripture in itself is a light and a lamp kindled, indeed the most splendid sun, yet just as the sun cannot reveal to a blind man what or of what kind it is, unless the blind man is illuminated from elsewhere, so Scripture cannot present itself to be recognised by any man in reality, unless the Holy Spirit illuminates the man's mind to see the dignity of Scripture. Note that the Pelagians did not admit this, because they did not concede that our nature is so corrupted that the mind cannot discern between the things that are of God [and those that are not of God], between the doctrines of men, whenever they are set before it. Nor can anyone be persuaded by any arguments, whether drawn from antiquity, from the agreement of the Scriptures, from the miracles by which Holy Scripture was confirmed, from the fulfilment of its prophecies, or from any other place, that these books are God-breathed [θεοπνεύστους], and the others not, without the Holy Spirit. As to their majesty and heavenly nature, to use the words of Tuissi, those destitute of the light of the Spirit do not indeed notice it in vain, but not at all. <i>The authority of Scripture</i>, says Bucer, <i>is not recognised even at all except through the Spirit of Christ.</i> <i>Indeed no one denies this</i>, adds Morus, <i>but he says it sufficiently generously.</i> Camero says: <i>the light which gently suffuses the mind and by which the eyes of the mind are opened.</i> <i>For blind men do not even look at the light of the sun</i>, so Whitaker says, <i>nor do those whose minds are not divinely illuminated look at the splendour of the Scriptures.... No one is so blind that he does not, even against his will, see some sparks of this light shining forth in the Scriptures. But this is of little account, and avails only for destruction, unless God opens the eyes of the mind and kindles in us a new light.</i>
