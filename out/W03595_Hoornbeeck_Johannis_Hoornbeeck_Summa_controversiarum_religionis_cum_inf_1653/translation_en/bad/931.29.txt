Simultaneous or cumulative marriage seems to be meant here, because the canon speaks of being entangled with them in embraces [συμπλοκαῖς], that is, with two marriages at once. Whatever the case with the Apostle's passage, the Greeks have always been harsher toward digamists and toward trigamists or polygamists in succession, imposing on them the ecclesiastical penalty of removal from the holy communion. The 111th canon of the Synod of Neocaesarea is perhaps to be understood in this sense. Basil, however, in his canonical letter to Amphilochius, canon 4, expressly places a digamist outside communion for a year, sometimes for two years, and a trigamist often for three, four, or even five years. They did not altogether exclude such people from the church, though they were kept from the holy communion. They were allowed to stand among the hearers [ἀκροάσεως], so that they remained among the hearers for two or three years; then they were allowed to prostrate themselves [ὑποπίπτοντες] among the faithful, yet they still had to stand [ἀπὸ χαλᾶς τῆς ἀγαθῆς χρονίας] for a long time, until, once their repentance was proved, they were admitted to participation in communion in their proper place. A fourth marriage, however, they condemned not only ecclesiastically but also politically, by a decree proclaimed every year in July from the platform, in this definition of Constantine Porphyrogenitus: "Let no one dare to fall into marriage [πίπτει γάμον μηδένι τολμῆσαι], but if anyone at all [ἀλλ' εἴ τις ἀπόλιπον πάντως]..." and, if anyone should wish to enter into such a union, he is to be deprived of every ecclesiastical assembly and kept away from entering the holy temple, for as long as he remains in that union. For this, they say, has seemed good even to the holy Fathers who went before us. We, indeed, making the sentence still plainer, cast him out as alien to the Christian commonwealth. As for trigamists, they limit the matter so that if a man has passed his fortieth year and has children from his earlier marriages, he is by no means permitted to proceed to a third marriage; but if he has no children, a trigamist is subject to the penalty of abstention from the holy communion for up to five years. He who is thirty years old and has taken children from earlier marriages, and who joins himself to a third wife, is subject to excommunication until the fourth year; but if he has no children, some leniency is granted to him, and he is nonetheless punished with a lighter penalty. Everything is rigid beyond fairness, since they everywhere practised a harsher discipline where none, or a milder one, ought to have been applied. So it should now seem less surprising to someone that the Greeks grant fewer dispensations in matters of bigamy, although it is read in the Confession of the Ethiopian patriarch made by Christophorus Licanatus, or Zaga Zabo, that the patriarch sometimes dispensed even in this matter for the public good. Balsamon, on canon 16 of the Apostles, also indicates that some bigamists have sometimes been promoted to higher orders, contrary to the canons. In blessing a marriage the Greeks use a crowning, whence the rites of "releasing" [ἀπολυτρώσεις] and "crowning" [στεφανώσεις] in their Liturgy, or euchologion, when the priest first places crowns on the bridegroom and then on the bride, with a solemn blessing, and on the eighth day they remove the same crowns again. But in the case of bigamists they omit this. The priest indeed blesses their marriages, using the formulas and prayers common for this purpose, but he does not crown bigamists; nor is this office performed during the celebration of the sacred rites, as on other occasions, and a bigamist is not admitted to it unless the period of repentance has been completed. Nor, however, is that observance of the crowns universal.
