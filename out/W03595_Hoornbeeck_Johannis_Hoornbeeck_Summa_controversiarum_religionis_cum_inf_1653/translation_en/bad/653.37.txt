[1] <i>Indeed, we hand down a shameful example to posterity when we would rather willingly throw away all our freedom than prick the feelings of one man with some slight offence. But he has a vehement temperament and violent impulses. As if this vehemence would not rather exult while everyone indulges it and permits everything! If this example of tyranny has already emerged in the first beginnings of the reborn Church, what will happen soon, once affairs have collapsed for the worse? Let us therefore lament the calamity of the Church, and not merely swallow this grief in silence among ourselves, but dare at last to utter a free groan. What if, by the Lord's permission, you have been driven into these straits, so that at last a fuller confession on this matter is wrung from you? I freely admit that what you teach is most true, and I praise your prudence and moderation, since you have hitherto striven to call men's minds back from contention by a gentle manner of teaching. But while you shun this place as if it were some rock, lest you incur some offence, you leave many people in suspense and perplexity, who ask from you something more certain on which they may rest. It is, moreover, as I recall telling you once before, not honourable enough for us to hesitate even to commit to ink that doctrine which many saints have not hesitated to leave attested by their own blood. God therefore now wishes perhaps to open to you the way to this full and solid explanation of your mind, so that those doubters who hang upon your authority (and I know there are very many of them) may not remain in doubt forever. I say this not so much to urge you as to console you. In the same way he wrote to him in the year MDLV, on the third day before the Nones of March: "Concerning [Περὶ τῆς ἀξιαλγείας] the inner feeling of your prudence has become known to me, which you do not conceal in your letters. But that excessive dullness of yours displeases me, by which the madness of those men is not only fostered but even daily increased, whom you see rushing so wantonly to the ruin of the whole Church." In the same year, on the tenth day before the Kalends of September: "What is plausible or odious to the world is not hidden from me. But nothing is more important to me than to follow the rule prescribed by the Master. Nor do I doubt that this frankness will in the end be more pleasing to the pious and sound than a pliable and soft manner of teaching, which brings with it empty fear. As to what you acknowledge you owe to God and the Church, I beg you to pay it off as soon as possible. I do not dwell on this point because I trust that, by unloading a part of the odium upon yourself, I shall be relieved by just as much. Rather, if it were allowed, whatever burden now lies upon you I would, out of my love and respect for you, be ready to take upon my own shoulders. But it is for you to weigh this, since I, as an adviser, would not approach unless you free the pious men who look to you from their doubt in good time; you will hardly ever be able to pay it off." Again, in the year MDLVII, on the third day before the Nones of August: "And you must indeed now see to it in good time, lest excessive silence bring you dishonour among posterity. But if, while those hippocentaurs stab you on every side with their weapons, you wait, there is again to be feared that the confession which so great a necessity has wrung from you may be judged untimely. What if death should overtake you? Will they not cry out that all your authority would perish and your credibility be abolished, because you were servilely timid? That you should hasten to wipe away this infamy I need not be told at length." That Melanchthon was more hesitant in this business is plain; but I thought these things should be copied out all the more gladly from Calvin's letters to him, because they have been neglected by others, and Peucer exhibited only one of those letters. Thus from them one may most thoroughly see that the mind and opinion of Melanchthon could be the same as Calvin's, for which purpose he laboured notably, so that they should be, or be made, [ὁμόφρονες] of one mind everywhere, even in those matters in which Melanchthon used a different and diverse manner of teaching from Calvin's, as in the business of free will and divine election, about which he admonishes him in various letters here and gently reproves him. Indeed I desire that we should agree so thoroughly among ourselves that no appearance of dissension should show even in a single word. But it is for you rather to go before me than for me to look to what pleases me. You see how familiarly I deal with you; yet I do not fear that I exceed the bounds, for I know how much I am permitted with you, given your singular goodwill towards me. With Dr Martin there was rather more difficulty; for as far as I could understand from memory and from the letters of certain people, the mind of that man could scarcely be irritated for a slight cause. Accordingly, the messenger will show you the letters I wrote to him, so that, having inspected them, you may direct the whole business by your counsel.</i> He indicates the letter to Luther written by himself, which the most distinguished man Alexander Morus published after his oration on Calvin, given to him at the same time, in MDXLV, on the twelfth day before the Kalends of February, when he had sent that letter to Melanchthon <i>together with the book on the Libertines</i>, asking their judgement on the matter, because there were in France (as he testifies to both Luther and Melanchthon) men who put forward Luther's authority so that it would be lawful for them to frequent the sacred rites of the Papists, even though they wished to be regarded as Reformed. I see that Melanchthon's reply to Calvin is recorded in a letter from the latter to him, in MDXLV, on the twenty-eighth day of June. Whether Luther also replied, I do not know, unless that letter of Melanchthon was common to both, about which I greatly doubt, because he does not speak in the plural when he gives thanks for it, nor does he mention Luther, or write that greetings or thanks should be returned, which I think a man otherwise most courteous would not have omitted. Leonhard Hutter, in <i>On the Origin of the Book of Concord</i>, against Hospinian, chapter 11, to prove the (as he puts it) collusions of Melanchthon with Calvin, adduces from Melanchthon's <i>second Defence</i> against Westphal, in the year MDLV, these fairly clear challenges of Calvin to Melanchthon's opinion: "If Philip should declare by a single word that I deviate from his mind, I will at once desist." Again, in the last Admonition, to the same: "If I should discover by chance that the name of Philip has been abused, I will refuse no marks of disgrace." And again: "I have said, and indeed a hundred times, and if need be I confirm it, that Philip cannot be torn from me in this cause (about the Sacrament) any more than from my own bowels, although he, fearing the thunderbolt that threatened from violent men (those who knew Luther's fans will understand what I mean), did not always set forth his opinion as openly as I would have wished."
