[1] 17. But from contracts properly so called I turn to quasi-contracts, and in particular to tutelage or curatorship. Here the following questions must be examined. If a tutor must be given to a ward, does the magistrate of the place where the ward has his domicile give him, or the magistrate of the place where the ward's immovable goods lie? I answer: although as a rule the tutor is given by the magistrate of the place where the ward has his domicile, where his parents lived, it is also held that one who appoints a tutor has appointed him chiefly for the person, not for the property. Hence one who is simply given a tutor is understood to be given for all his affairs, even those situated in different provinces. This was generally the rule under Roman law, by which the magistrates of different provinces were all subject to one emperor. Yet lest the judge of the domicile seem to have done something outside his territory, this does not prejudice the judge of the place where some of the ward's goods lie, and he may properly appoint a tutor there for the ward in respect of those goods, namely the immovable ones. Hence too, if there is a dispute about alienating a minor's estates, and they lie in another province, the tutor who was appointed in the place of domicile will act more safely if he takes care to have the decree confirmed by both judges, both of the ward's domicile and of the place where the property lies. <i>Instit. qui testam. tutor. §. 4. l. 12. l. 13. l. 14.
