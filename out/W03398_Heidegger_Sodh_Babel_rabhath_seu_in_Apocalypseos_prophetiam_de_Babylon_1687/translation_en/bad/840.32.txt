[1] LXII. Whether the final day of this destruction is near or still far off, the day on which Babylon, that is, the whole polity of the Babylonish Hierarchy, will be destroyed, and so according to those prophetic words of the most pious and most brave Emperor Frederick II:

[2] Rome, long tottering and driven by long errors, will fall, and will cease to be the head of the world;

[3] It does not seem to be the task of this place to investigate this, since by the common consent of all, the Prophecy of Babylon, which alone we have undertaken to explain here, sets no fixed marks of the time, year or day on which that dreadful judgment will come. From other prophecies, in which the duration of Antichrist seems to be defined, the most learned men of our times have tried to search out and dig up the moment of that time. And truly the prophecy of Daniel 12:4 about the last times is fulfilled every day, which speaks thus: "At the time of the end [ךיכ שטוי] many shall search, and knowledge shall be increased"; that is, they will compare the prophecies with the event. And just as "the prophets searched what time or what manner of time the Spirit of Christ, which was in them, signified, testifying beforehand the sufferings of Christ and the glories that would follow" (1 Pet. 1:10, 11), so these men also eagerly labour to draw out of the prophecies the time into which the time of Antichrist and his downfall will fall. In this search some men, otherwise not bad, have gone somewhat unhappily astray; the event itself, which is usually the key to interpretation, has taught this, and after others the celebrated Theophilus Spizelius has related it in his twofold Diatribe on the Final Destruction of Rome. Among these are counted Martin Harky, the trumpeter of the holy war's trumpet, a Belgian; and an anonymous Englishman, author of a book entitled "The Final Ruin of Rome, A.D. 1666, and the Ends of the World under A.D. 1711", who, when that very year 1666 was at hand, gave me the occasion of writing a little book "On the Signs of the Times", in which I set out my notion of the time of Babylon's fall, so intricately that no reasons have so far appeared for which I think the statement ought to be changed. Briefly I repeat: nearly all the things which, according to all the prophecies of the Old and New Testaments, ought to precede that destruction and make it clear as it draws near, have been shown by the event itself, and perhaps little else remains than that God should complete all his work on Mount Sion. The series of the Prophecy of Babylon, which we have treated up to now, demonstrates this most clearly on its own. For the last sign of the swift destruction is laid down in this, that Babylon, after her former fall, has striven with all her powers to regain her former greatness, and has advanced so far that, with those who came out of her crushed and nearly reduced to nothing, she sings her boastful triumph song: "I sit a queen, I am no widow, and I shall see no mourning" (Rev. 18:7). When after her former fall did she sing this with a louder voice than today? That the seven seals, trumpets and vials portend, we have shown there, that nearly all of them have been fulfilled up to the destruction of Great Babylon. God has not failed the Church until now, whom he has more than once raised up with his present and powerful help. Yet among those who call upon Christ together there does not appear that harmony which God requires of them, and which all good men rightly desire as worthy of the most fuller Gospel [πλείονι]. Therefore, lest it seem strange if the arm of God's people is weakened even more, and, according to the prophecy of Isaiah cited above, the whole earth again play the harlot with Tyre, so that the Babylonian song "I sit a queen, &c." can be declaimed with a more distended throat. For this is the constant rule of the Gospel, that the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against those who have used it unworthily and hold the truth in unrighteousness. Let us therefore wait, possessing our souls in patience, and let us stand as on a watch-tower, observing what God does; if perhaps, while we watch and observe like those keeping vigil at the last watch of the night, the dawn may break. So Isaiah 21:6, when a watchman had been set on the tower by God's command, to report what he saw, is said to have cried like a lion: "Lord, I stand continually on the watch-tower by day, and I persevere at my post every night; and behold, a chariot of a man, a team of horsemen." Then the Lord answered in verse 9: "Fallen, fallen is Babylon." This fall is clear at least in its [ιzzoπτωσι], and as an example of the new Babylon, or the last offshoot of Babylon, from Rev. 14:8 and 18:2. Therefore, whether God does unexpected things, and things which do not seem to portend the fall of Babylon, as when he allows Babylon to triumph and the hand of the people of the saints is crushed more and more; or whether he for a while suspends his work, and for that reason the watchman, as in that half-hour's silence of Rev. 8:1, can indicate nothing, this very thing must be held to be a sign of the approaching fall of Babylon. Perhaps too, as with Jerusalem, so the destruction of Babylon will have a shortening of the days [ἡμερῶν κολάσεως] on account of the elect, who cry to God day and night, and on account of the blood of the martyrs, who continually demand vengeance.
