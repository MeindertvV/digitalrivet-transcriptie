[1] It is <i>dyscheresia</i> [δυσχέρεια]. Theophrastus distinguishes subtly between δυσχέρεια and ἀηδία, which is treated in the following chapter. Both words denote the awkwardness and perversity of certain people in company and in the ordinary business of life, who think nothing of adopting a manner, a dress, habits, and finally speech that rightly earns them weariness, hatred and, by the superstition of the ancients, execration as of bad omen, from those they live among. And τὸ δυσχερὲς and τὸ ἀηδὲς seem to differ more by degree (τῷ μᾶλλον καὶ τῷ ἦττον) than by kind, although Theophrastus describes them as different kinds, for I will not say he defines them. Indeed, in this very chapter he himself calls the δυσχερής man ἀηδής, which shows how small the interval is between these two vices. Yet the one called δυσχέρεια is far more serious and repellent. I think δυσχέρεια is most conveniently rendered in Latin as <i>foulness and filthiness</i>, for those who translate it as uncleanness have said too little and have not captured the force of the Greek word. Properly δυσχερής, as the word itself indicates and as the learned Aulus Gellius hints, means what is intractable, troublesome and odious. Thus δυσχερῆ ἕλκη [troublesome ulcers] are old, fetid sores, as when Dio Chrysostom says of Philoctetes that no one was willing to nurse him in his disease, which was δυσχερής [τὸν δυσχερῆ τῆς νόσου]. Plutarch says that some diseases are dishonourable and foul [ἀτίμως καὶ μιαρᾶς], such as those mentioned here; such indeed were once the Laspodiae and Damasiae of the Athenians, whom Aristophanes and Eupolis lampooned for their foulness and stench. The verb δυσχεραίνει is used in the same way, as in Plutarch's Artaxerxes, where the body is said to have been made loathsome (ἐδυσχέρανεν) because of the outrage. This word signifies not only foulness but also, as we said, a bad omen, for in Demosthenes and the other Attic writers τὸ δυσχερὲς generally means something ill-omened and blasphemous, as Ulpian explains. Among the Latins, Seneca calls a foul variegation of white [ἀλφός] a <i>foul variety of white</i>, in the third book of the Natural Questions. That δυσχερής was a disease is also shown by the passage of Plutarch just cited.
