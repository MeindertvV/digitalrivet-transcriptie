[1] 4. This burden upon the mind forces the tongue to vent its sorrow in the saddest accents of most doleful <i>outcries</i>; their whole language is lamentation. But when the pangs of their agonies come upon them (for their distresses have their fits), then they speak in the bitterness of their souls. Oh! (said <i>Bainham</i>) <i>I would not, for all the world's good, feel such a hell in my conscience again.</i> One (formerly mentioned), in these distresses, cries out: <i>Woe, woe, woe! A woeful, a wretched, a forsaken woman!</i> It would surely have made a man's hair stand upright for dread to have heard Spira roaring out that terrible sentence: <i>How dreadful is it to fall into the hands of the living God?</i> Or to have heard his reply to him who told of his being at <i>Venice, O cursed day!</i> (saith he) <i>O cursed day! O that I had never gone thither, would God I had then died!</i> etc. The like outcries David often had: <i>Psal. 22.1. My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?</i> Why art thou so far from helping me, <i>and from the words of my roaring?</i> And Heman: <i>Psal. 88. 14. Lord, why castest thou off my soul, why hidest thou thy face from me?</i> It is true, David's and Heman's words have a better complexion than those others last mentioned, but their disquiet of heart seems at times to have urged their <i>expressions</i> with impetuous violence, as those passages seem to say: <i>Psal. 38. 8. I have roared by reason of the disquietness of my heart. Psal. 32. 3. My bones waxed old through my roaring all the day long. Job 3. 24. My roarings are poured out like water.</i> If their lamentations were turned into roarings, and those roarings were like the breaking in of a flood, and that flood of so <i>long</i> continuance that it <i>dried</i> up the marrow of the bones, we may safely imagine that they were not so much at leisure to <i>order</i> their words, but that their tongues might speak in that <i>dialect</i> which is proper to astonishment and distresses.
