[1] 1. Where there is much <i>discomposure</i>, there is much <i>sin</i>. If there is no lack of iniquity <i>in a multitude of words</i>, then there is far more in a multitude of <i>unruly thoughts</i>. A disturbed spirit is like <i>troubled</i> water: all the <i>mud</i> that lay at the bottom is stirred up and <i>mixes</i> itself with the thoughts. If any injury or loss troubles the mind, all the thoughts are tinged with anger, pride, impatience, or whatever root of bitterness was in the heart before. We do not view these events singly as the issues of wise <i>providence</i>; ordinarily we consider them as done by such <i>instruments</i>, and against our <i>selves</i> as malicious, spiteful, causeless, ungrateful wrongs. Then we give ourselves too great a liberty to rage, to plot revenge, to threaten, to reproach, and what not. And even if our disposition has no strong natural inclination to these distempers, the thoughts are quickly leavened by discomposure (this is the comparison the Apostle uses, <i>1 Cor. 5.8.</i>, to express the power of malice, which is a usual attendant of this condition, to infect all the imaginations) and become <i>fermented</i> with a sharpness that makes them swell into excess and exorbitance. Hence proceed revilings, quarrelings, and so on. When the tongue is thus stirred, it is <i>a fire, a world of iniquity</i> (producing more sins than can be counted); <i>it defileth the whole body</i> (engaging all the faculties in headlong pursuit), <i>Jam. 3.6.</i>
