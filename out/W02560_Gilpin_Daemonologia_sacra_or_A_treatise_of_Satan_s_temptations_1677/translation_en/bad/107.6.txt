[1] So those who would sincerely seek the truth, in my judgment, do not take the best course when they immediately engage themselves in a public dispute; for the usual heats that arise in a contention turn their minds away from a true impartiality, and the dust they stir up blinds their eyes, so that they do not discern things truly. Let us look into this device of the Devil, of engaging pride by disputation, and through it the judgment. First, we find that when a spirit of contending is aroused, certain truths are neglected as to their improvement and practice; for so much of the strength of the soul is spent upon disputable questions that little is left for weightier matters. Secondly, in disputes men's reputations are so involved that it is most difficult to keep a faithful regard for truth, especially where the disputes are carried on with insults and abuse. Those who by calm handling might be brought to acknowledge a mistake will scarcely come near that point of candour when they must be called fool, knave, or ass for their pains. Hence, ordinarily (though they profess otherwise), men seek victory rather than truth. Thirdly, in disputes pride and passion are usually heightened, and the stronger the passions are, the weaker the judgment. Eager altercations bring confusion both upon the matter under dispute and upon the understanding that ought to judge. Fourthly, in the heat of disputation, when the mind is inflamed, men usually behave like those in a brawl, where they snatch and throw whatever comes to hand, and never mind where it hits; they will affirm or deny anything that may seem in any way to get them off. Fifthly, these assertions, once affirmed, must be maintained, and so errors and contentions increase without end. Disputes fix a man in his persuasion and, as it were, tie him to the stake, so that, right or wrong, he will go through with it. Sixthly, some dispute in jest against their present judgment, and yet at last dispute themselves into a belief in what they wantonly affirmed at first; as some tell lies so long that at length they believe them to be true. Seventhly, a sadder mischief often follows a disputatious humour, which is the hazard of losing <i>all truth</i>; men dispute so long that they suspect <i>all things</i>, and after a long trade in <i>scepticism</i>, they turn <i>atheists</i>.
