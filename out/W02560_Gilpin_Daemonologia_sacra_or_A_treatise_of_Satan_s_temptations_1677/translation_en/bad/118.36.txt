[1] 3. When the heart is weighed down by inward griefs, it is ready to accept the <i>worst interpretation</i> the Devil can put on the <i>providences</i> of God. The many instances in Scripture, and the gracious <i>promises</i> made to those who <i>walk in darkness and see no light</i>, warn us strongly against drawing bad conclusions about God's dealings. They tell us that God, by design, for our <i>trial</i> and our <i>profit</i>, often hides his face <i>for a moment</i>, when his purpose is to <i>bind us up with everlasting compassions.</i> The Devil, however, works to turn the sorrows of the mind to a quite contrary construction. Where the afflicted should say, <i>Sorrow may endure for a night, but joy will come in the morning</i>, or that <i>for a little while God hath hidden himself</i>, he persuades them to say, <i>this darkness shall never pass away.</i> If the grief is small, he drives them on to a fearful expectation of worse, as he did with Hezekiah, Isa. 38.13: <i>I reckoned till morning, that as a lion, so will he break all my bones; from day even to night wilt thou make an end of me.</i> If God means to teach us through inward sorrows, whether our pride of heart, carelessness, neglect of dependence upon him, the bitterness of sin, or something similar, the Devil will make us believe (and we are only too ready to agree with him) that God declares open war against us and resolves never to own us again. So Job, chap. 19. 6: <i>Know now that God hath overthrown me, and compassed me with his net.</i> How often did he complain, <i>thou hast made me as thy mark, thou hast broken me asunder, thou hast taken me by my neck and shaken me to pieces?</i> So also Heman, Psal. 88. 14: <i>Why castest thou off my soul? why hidest thou thy face from me?</i>
