[1] Thirdly, the <i>advantage</i> the Devil gains in working <i>delusion</i> through this pretence is a strong incentive for him to practise it. For inspirations, visions, voices, impulses, dreams and revelations are things in which wicked <i>impostors</i> may play the <i>counterfeit</i> in many ways and by many artifices without being detected. It is <i>easy</i> to prophesy <i>false dreams</i> and to <i>say, Thus saith the Lord</i>, when <i>they only lie, and the Lord never sent them nor commanded them</i>. Indeed it is <i>easy</i>, by <i>tricks</i> and <i>illusions</i>, to lend to their <i>schemes</i> a credit and honour which they could never win by their bare <i>assertions</i>, backed by all their art of seeming earnestness. The <i>inventions</i> of men who have been successful in this deceit in the past, now <i>laid open</i> to our knowledge, may make us more wary in our trust. Among the <i>heathen</i> you may find notable examples of deceit of this kind. The story of <i>Hanno</i> and <i>Psappho</i> is commonly known: they tamed birds and taught them to speak, <i>Hanno and Psappho are gods</i>, and then set them free, so that men hearing such <i>strange voices</i> from birds in the woods might imagine that these men had been declared <i>gods</i> by special revelation. <i>Mahomet's</i> device of making a <i>dove</i> come frequently to his ear (which he did by training her to pick corn out of it) served him as proof, among the common onlookers who did not know the true cause, of his immediate inspiration by the <i>angel Gabriel</i>, who (as he told them) whispered in his ear in the <i>shape of a dove</i>. He practised a similar trickery to confirm the truth of his <i>Alcoran</i>, by making a bull (previously taught to come at a call or signal) come to him with a chapter upon its horns. <i>Hector Boece</i> tells of a similar stratagem by a King of Scots, who, to rouse his fainting subjects against the <i>Picts</i> who had beaten them, had a man clothed in the <i>shining skins of fishes</i>, and carrying <i>rotten wood</i> (which, like a glow-worm at night, gives a faint light), come among them in the dark, and speak through a <i>reed</i> or hollow trunk (so that the voice might not seem human), urging them to a vigorous attack. They took this to be an <i>angel</i> bringing them this command from heaven, and accordingly they fought and prevailed. Crafty <i>Benedict</i> (who was afterwards pope under the name of <i>Boniface the Eighth</i>) made simple <i>Celestine the Fifth</i> give up the <i>papacy</i>, by conveying to him a voice through a reed to this effect: <i>Celestine, Celestine, renounce the papacy, give it up, if you would be saved; the burden is beyond your strength, etc.</i> The silly man, taking this for a revelation from heaven, quitted his chair and left it to that crafty fox <i>Benedict</i>. Not many years ago the same trick was played in this country on <i>a man of revelations</i>, who called himself <i>David in Spirit</i>. When he had wearied his host with a long stay, he got rid of his company (as I was credibly informed) by a stratagem which he perceived would suit the man's conceitedness well: through a <i>reed</i> in the <i>night time</i> he tells him that he must go into <i>Wales</i>, or some such country, and preach the gospel there. The next morning the man <i>avows a revelation from God</i> to go elsewhere, and so departs. These instances show you how cunningly a cheating knave may carry on a pretence of revelation or vision. And yet this is not all the advantage the Devil has in this matter, though it is an advantage he sometimes uses when he is provided with suitable instruments. But he works most <i>dangerously</i> when he so acts upon men that they <i>themselves</i> believe they have visions, raptures and revelations; for some are <i>really</i> persuaded that it is so with them. Nor is it strange that men should be deluded into thinking they hear and see what they do not; in <i>fevers, frenzies</i> and <i>madness</i> we clearly see this to be so. And who can <i>convince</i> such persons of their <i>mistakes</i>, when with the highest confidence they insist <i>that they are not deceived?</i> Shall we think it strange that Satan has ways of conveying false impressions to men's minds? No, surely: do we not see that the <i>senses</i> may be cheated, and that the <i>fancies</i> of men may be corrupted? Is it not easy for him to convey <i>voices</i> to the ear, or <i>shapes</i> and representations to the eye? And in such cases, what can ordinarily hinder a belief that they hear or see such things? But he does not always need to work upon the fancy through the senses. If he has the advantage of a <i>crazy, distempered fancy</i> (as he commonly has in <i>melancholy persons</i>), he can so <i>strongly fix</i> his suggestions upon them, and so <i>effectually</i> set the fancy to work to <i>embrace</i> them, that without any appearance of madness they will persuade themselves that they have <i>discoveries</i> from God, <i>impulses</i> by his Spirit, <i>scriptures</i> set upon their hearts, and what not. And because they <i>feel</i> the workings of these things within them, it is impossible to make them even <i>suspect</i> that they are deceived. Just consider the power of <i>any fancy</i> in a <i>melancholic</i> person, and you may easily grasp how Satan works in such delusions. Melancholy strangely <i>perverts</i> the imagination, and will beget in men wonderful misapprehensions, and sometimes it so bewitches them into a peremptory, <i>uncontrollable</i> belief in their fancy. It is a vehement, confident humour, whichever way it takes; the imagination thus corrupted has an enormous <i>strength</i>, so that if it fixes upon things however absurd or irrational, it cannot be reduced by the strongest reasons. If such a man conceits himself <i>dead</i>, or that he is <i>transformed</i> into a <i>wolf</i> or cat, or that he is made of <i>glass</i> (as many in this distemper have done), no persuasion to the contrary can take hold of him. Now if this humour is taken up with <i>divine matters</i> (as usually it is, for it has a <i>natural inclination to religious things</i>), it still acts with <i>fierceness</i> and <i>confidence</i>; and there are often many things accompanying such actings that, if it misconceives inspiration or prophecy, the parties themselves are not only <i>bound up</i> under that persuasion, but even <i>unwary spectators</i> are deluded. For sometimes a melancholy imagination is not <i>wholly</i> corrupt, but only in respect of some <i>one</i> or <i>two</i> particulars, whilst in <i>other</i> things it acts <i>regularly</i>. Then neither they nor others (who are unacquainted with such cases) are so apt to suspect that they are <i>mistaken</i> in these things, while they act <i>rationally</i> and <i>soberly</i> in <i>other</i> matters. Sometimes they have vehement <i>fits of surprisal</i> (for the humour has its ebbings and flowings), and this gives them occasion to apprehend that <i>something</i> does <i>supernaturally</i> act upon them or raise them. Then when the things they say are, in their <i>matter</i>, of <i>religious concern</i> and <i>odd notions</i> (for the humour flies high and does not confine itself to ordinary things), and withal uttered in <i>Scripture rhetoric</i>, and with <i>fervency</i> and urgency of spirit, and when these things <i>concur</i>, there is such an appearance of <i>inspiration</i> that the parties themselves and others rest fully persuaded that it is so.
