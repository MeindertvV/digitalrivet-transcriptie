[1] Secondly, especially if we consider that God himself took <i>this course</i> to make his mind known to men: his prophets were <i>divinely inspired</i>, and the Scriptures were not of <i>any private interpretation</i>. The <i>words</i> that the penmen of Scripture wrote were not <i>interpretations</i> of their own <i>private thoughts</i>; for prophecy came not in old time <i>by the will of man</i>, but <i>holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost</i> (2 Pet. 1.20, 21). Now, though the prophecies of Scripture are <i>sealed</i>, and nothing more is to be <i>added</i> to them on any pretence whatsoever, yet since promises are left us of <i>the giving of the Spirit</i> and of <i>being taught and led by the Spirit</i>, it is an easy matter for Satan to beguile men into expecting prophetic <i>inspirations</i>, and into believing whatever is pretended to be such. For not all men do understand, or will understand, that these promises of the Spirit have no intention of <i>new</i> and <i>extraordinary</i> immediate <i>revelations</i>, but only of the <i>effectual application</i> of what is <i>already revealed</i> in Scripture. This <i>kind</i> of revelation we acknowledge and teach, and it is far enough from <i>enthusiasm</i> (that is, a pretended revelation of <i>new</i> truths); and we have reason to maintain <i>that inward persuasions without the outward word are to be avoided as Satan's cozenages</i>. But for all this, when men's minds are set a-gadding, and they meet with those who magnify their own <i>dreams</i> and call their fancies <i>visions</i>, the suitableness of this to their humour makes them reject our <i>interpretations</i> of these promises as false, and persuade themselves that they are to be understood of such inspirations <i>as the prophets of old had</i>; and then they presently conclude that they must <i>believe</i> them, lest otherwise they should <i>resist the Holy Ghost</i>.
