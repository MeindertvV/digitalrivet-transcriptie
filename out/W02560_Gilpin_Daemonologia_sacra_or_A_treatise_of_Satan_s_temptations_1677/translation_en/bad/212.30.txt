[1] 3. It is another part of Satan's cunning, while they are thus at a stand, to urge upon them a <i>possibility</i>, nay, a <i>probability</i>, that they have <i>mistaken</i> themselves by passing too favourable an opinion formerly of their actions. To confirm them in this apprehension: (1) he sets before them the consideration of the <i>deceitfulness</i> of the heart, which (being above all things deceitful and desperately wicked, beyond ordinary discovery) makes a fair way for the entertainment of a suspicion of <i>self-delusion</i> in all the former hopes which a man has had of himself. Satan will plainly speak it: <i>Thou hast had some thoughts and workings of mind towards God, but seeing they bear so great a disproportion to rule and example, and fall so far short of common graces, it is more than probable that such poor, weak, confused appearances are nothing. How knowest thou that thy adherence to, and practice of, the commands and services of God are any more than the product of education, the prevalence of custom, or the impressions of moral persuasion? How dost thou know that thy desires after God, and thy delight in him, are any more than the products of natural principles, influenced by an historical faith in Scripture doctrine?</i> It is often enough for Satan merely to hint this. A suspicious heart, as it were greedy of its own misery, catches at all things that tell against it, and hence complains that it has no grace, because it sees no visible fruits, or makes no sufficient appearance at all times when opposed or resisted; or because it lacks sensible progress, or yields not the summer fruits of praises, rejoicings and delights in God; or because it seems not to meet with remarkable improvement in ordinances; or because it cannot produce tears and stir the affections; and because the party does not know but that his heart may have deceived him in all that he has done. The devil endeavours to confirm this further (2) by a consideration of the <i>seeming</i> holiness and graces of such as believed themselves to be children of God, and were generally so reputed by others, who yet, after a glorious profession, turned apostates. This being so great and undeniable an instance of the heart's deceitfulness, makes the poor tempted party conclude that he is certainly no true convert.
