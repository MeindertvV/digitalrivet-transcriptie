[1] 3. Satan also draws arguments from the <i>sins</i> of God's children, and his great skill here is to exaggerate their faults so that they look like those offences which Scripture, by a <i>special exception</i>, excludes from pardon. The Apostle, 1 John 5.16, speaks of a <i>sin that is unto death</i>, that is, a sin which, if a man commits it, he cannot escape eternal death, and therefore he would not have such a sinner prayed for. (That the Papists' distinction between venial and mortal sins is not meant here, some of the Papists themselves admit.) What he means by that sin he does not tell us, since it is known well enough from other Scriptures. The mark of <i>unpardonableness</i> is indeed attached to sins under <i>several denominations</i>. Christ pronounces the <i>sin against the Holy Ghost</i> unpardonable, Matt. 12.31. <i>Total apostasy</i> from the truth of the gospel the Apostle says no less of, when he calls it <i>a drawing back to perdition</i>, Heb. 10.39. Whether these are all one, or whether there is any other kind of sin that cannot be forgiven besides that against the Holy Ghost, it is not our purpose to inquire. Whatever they are in themselves, Satan in this matter makes use of the texts that speak of them distinctly, as we shall presently see. But besides these, the Scriptures speak of some who were <i>given up to vile affections</i> <i>and to a reprobate mind</i>, Rom. 1.26, 28, and of others who were given up <i>to hardness of heart</i>, Matt. 13.14, Acts 28.26. Now, whoever they are of whom these things may justly be affirmed, they are certainly miserable, hopeless wretches. Here, then, is Satan's cunning: if he can make any child of God believe that he has done any <i>such act</i>, or <i>acts</i> of sin, that may bring him within the compass of these Scriptures, then he insults over him and tells him again and again that he is cut off for ever.
