[1] 'Tis evident then that Satan by this artifice is accustomed to put a stamp of divine warrant upon his adulterated coin; and if we look into his practice, we shall in all ages find him at this work. Among the heathens he frequently gained a reputation for his superstitious, idolatrous worship by this device: the men of greatest note among them feigned a <i>spiritual commerce</i> with the gods. Empedocles endeavoured to make the people believe that there was a kind of divinity in him, and, affecting to be esteemed more than a man, he cast himself into the fires of Mount Etna, so that they might suppose him to have been <i>taken up to the gods</i>. Pythagoras's fiction of a journey to Hell was on the same account. Philostratus and Cedrenus report no less of Apollonius, namely that he had <i>familiar converses</i> with their supposed deities; and they believed the like of their <i>Magi</i> and priests; insomuch that some cunning <i>politicians</i>, observing how the common people were under a deep reverence for such pretences, gave it out that they had received their <i>laws</i> by <i>divine inspirations</i>. Numa Pompilius feigned that he received his institutions from the nymph Egeria; Lycurgus from Apollo; Minos, the lawgiver of Crete, boasted that Jupiter was his familiar. Mahomet also speaks as high in this way as any: his Alcoran must be nothing less than a law received <i>from God</i>, and to that end he pretends a strange journey to Heaven and frequent converse with the angel Gabriel.
