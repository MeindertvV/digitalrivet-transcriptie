[1] 3. Having now confined the discourse to the <i>spiritual distresses</i> of God's children, that are not so oppressed with melancholy as to be misled by false imaginations, I must next offer, concerning these distresses, another observable <i>distinction</i>: namely, that they are either made up of <i>all the five</i> aforementioned ingredients, or of only <i>some of them</i>, and so may be called <i>total</i> or <i>partial</i>; though within each of these there may be great differences of degree. 1. Sometimes, then, the children of God may be brought into <i>total</i> distresses of conscience, even with <i>despair</i> and (what is more hideous) with <i>blasphemy</i>. This holds true if Mr. <i>Perkins</i>'s observation is right, who tells us that they may be so overcharged with sorrow as to cry out that they are damned, and to blaspheme God. And we have no reason to contradict it, when we observe how far <i>David</i> went in his haste, more than once. And, whatever private differences there may be between these and the reprobate in their agonies (differences there are, both in <i>God's design</i> and in <i>their hearts</i>, though they are not visible), yet if we compare the fears, troubles and speeches of the one and the other, there appears <i>little</i> or no difference that bystanders could certainly fix upon. If it seems harsh to anyone that so horrid a thing as <i>despair</i> should be charged upon the <i>elect</i> of God in the <i>worst</i> of their distresses, it will readily be answered: (1) that if we <i>do not suppose</i> this, we must suppose something <i>worse</i>. If we do not like to say that God's children may fall into <i>despair</i>, we must conclude (very uncharitably) that those <i>who fall</i> into despair are not God's children. (2) It is easy to imagine a difference between <i>partial</i> and <i>total</i> despair, between <i>imaginary</i> and <i>real</i>. The children of God, under strong perturbation of spirit, may imagine themselves to do what they do not, and so may bear <i>false witness</i> against themselves, professing that all their hope of salvation is lost, when yet the <i>root</i> of their hope may still remain in their hearts <i>undiscovered</i>. The <i>habit</i> may be there when all visible <i>acts</i> of it are for the <i>present</i> suspended, or so disguised in a crowd of confused expressions that they cannot be known. Or, if they have <i>real distrust</i> of their salvation, yet every <i>fit</i> of real diffidence is not <i>utter desperateness</i>; nor will it make a man <i>totally desperate</i>, any more than every <i>error</i>, even about <i>fundamentals</i>, makes a man a <i>heretic</i>. For as it must be a <i>pertinacious</i> error in fundamentals that makes a heretic, so it must be a <i>pertinacious diffidence</i> that makes a man truly desperate. (3) But sometimes the children of God have only <i>partial</i> distresses; that is, they may have a great measure of <i>some</i> of the ingredients, without a mixture of the rest. In particular, they may have a great measure of the <i>sense of divine wrath</i> and desertion <i>without despair</i>. The possibility of this is evident beyond exception in the example of our Blessed Saviour, when he cried out, <i>My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?</i> No one can ascribe despair to him without blasphemy; and if anyone should, the very words <i>My God, my God</i> (expressing his full and certain <i>hope</i>) directly contradict them. Such an instance of spiritual distress <i>without despair</i> I take Heman to be. How high his troubles were is abundantly testified in <i>Psal. 88.</i>, and yet that his hope was <i>not lost</i> appears not only from his prayer for relief in general (for hope is not utterly destroyed where the appointed means of help are carefully used), but from the particular <i>avowal</i> of his hope in God in the first verses of that Psalm: <i>O Lord God of my salvation, I have cried day and night before thee.</i>
