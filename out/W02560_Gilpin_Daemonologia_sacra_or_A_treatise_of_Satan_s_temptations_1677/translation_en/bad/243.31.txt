[1] 2. When they are <i>tired</i> out with private conflicts, and have no rest or respite from trouble, then at last they are forced <i>to speak</i>; and having once begun to open up their troubles, they care not who knows it. If there is some heinous sin at the bottom, their consciences are forced to <i>confess</i> it: wickedness (which was once sweet in his mouth) is <i>turned in his bowels; it is the gall of asps within him.</i> Thus God makes men vomit up what they had swallowed down. Terrors chase away all shame; they can now freely speak against their sin, with the highest aggravations. And if their consciences have no heinous crime to accuse them of in <i>particular</i>, yet in the <i>general</i> they will judge and condemn themselves, as the <i>most stubborn</i>, sinful, or <i>hardened</i> wretches, justly branded with indelible marks of the wrath of God. However, the distress becomes greater if they <i>truly</i> accuse themselves of some particular sin; that vomiting is not done without a <i>violence</i> offered to nature, which otherwise would cover its shame. It cannot be done without sickness, straining and torture; and when it is done, they take it for granted that everyone passes the same judgment upon them as they do upon themselves. And the <i>frequent</i> speaking doth <i>confirm</i> their minds in their fearful expectations. For what men <i>accustom</i> themselves to assert, that they more confidently <i>believe.</i> If they only complain of themselves in general, with any intention of gaining pity (as is usual for the distressed), yet while they cry out to others, <i>Is this nothing to you, all you that pass by? Is there any sorrow like unto my sorrow?</i> &c., still they think their <i>stroke is heavier than their groaning</i>, and their cry to others strongly fixes this apprehension in themselves, that none can be more miserable than they. Thus are they brought to the condition of <i>Job</i>, <i>Job 16.6. Though I speak, my grief is not assuaged; and though I forbear, what am I eased?</i>
