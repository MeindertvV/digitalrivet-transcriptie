[1] 3. From hence it follows that no counsel or advice can take effect with them. Excessive fears remove their souls so far from peace that they will not believe there is any hope for them, even when it is told them. The most compassionate and earnest admonitions of friends, the strongest arguments against despair, the clearest disclosures of the hopes that lie before them, and so on, accomplish little. While they are being spoken they may seem to relieve them a little, but the comfort does not stay with them; it is soon gone. Though they cannot answer the arguments brought forward for them, yet they cannot believe them, as if their souls were now deprived of all power to believe anything for their good. This matches the expression of Spira, in reply to his friends who laboured to comfort him: <i>I would believe comfort but cannot</i>; <i>I can believe nothing but what is contrary to my comfort</i>. Nay, when they are told that many others have been under the same dreadful apprehensions of everlasting misery and have at last been comforted (and by much experience we find that it is <i>the greatest ease to distressed souls</i> <i>to hear</i> (especially to speak with) <i>some who have been in the same case</i>, for this will often give <i>some hope</i> that they too may <i>at last</i> be comforted, when the most comforting <i>promises</i> of Scripture are a <i>terror</i> to them), yet this sometimes brings them no ease at all, because some are so <i>wholly</i> possessed by <i>unalterable</i> prejudice against themselves that they think <i>none</i> are, or ever were, <i>like</i> them. They compare themselves to Judas and Cain, and judge their iniquity to be aggravated by many circumstances, far beyond the pitch of theirs. Thus Spira judged of himself: <i>I tell you</i> (he says), <i>my case is my own; it is singular, none is like it.</i>
