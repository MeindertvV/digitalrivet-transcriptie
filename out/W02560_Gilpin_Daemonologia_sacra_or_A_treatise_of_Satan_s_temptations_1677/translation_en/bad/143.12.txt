Though God, out of his common bounty to mankind, has allowed him a comfortable being in the world, yet we find that generally the sons of men, under their various occupations and studies, are worn out with <i>vexations of spirit</i>. <i>Solomon</i> in <i>Ecclesiastes</i> sets this out at length in the various employments of men, not exempting the pursuit of <i>Wisdom</i> and knowledge (<i>chap. 1.18. In much wisdom is much grief, and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow</i>), nor pleasures, nor riches; for by all these he shows that a man is liable to disquiets. So the general account of man's life is just this: <i>Eccles. 2.23. All his days are sorrows, and his travail grief; yea, his heart taketh not rest in the night.</i> That it is so is attested by common experience beyond denial; but how it comes to be so is the question. It is either from <i>God</i> or from <i>Satan</i>, working by occasions upon our tempers. That it is not from God is evident: for though sorrow is part of that <i>Curse</i> which man was justly doomed to, yet he has appointed ways and means by which it might be so <i>mitigated</i> that it would be tolerable without <i>discomposure</i> of spirit. Therefore <i>Solomon</i>, designing in his <i>Ecclesiastes</i> to set forth the chief good, shows that felicity consists not in the common abuse of outward things (because that brings only vexation), but in the <i>fear of God</i> leading to future happiness, and in the meantime in a <i>thankful, comfortable use</i> of present things without anxiety of mind. Hence he fixes his conclusion as the result of his experience, and often repeats it: <i>There is nothing better for a man, than that he should eat and drink, and that he should make his soul enjoy good in his labour.</i> Eccles. 2.24, 3.12,13, 5.18,19. Not that <i>Solomon</i> plays the <i>Epicure</i>, giving advice <i>to eat and drink, for tomorrow we die</i>; nor that he speaks <i>deridingly</i> to those who seek their felicity in this life, as if he should say, <i>If ye do terminate your desires upon a terrene felicity, there is nothing better than to eat and drink</i>, etc. But he gives a serious, <i>positive</i> advice, of enjoying the things of this life with <i>cheerfulness</i>, which he affirms proceeds from the sole bounty of God, as his singular gift: <i>It is the gift of God</i>, Eccles. 3.13. It is our <i>portion</i>, that is, our allowance, Eccles. 5.19; for these two expressions [<i>our portion</i> and <i>God's gift</i>] are of the same signification with <i>Solomon</i> here. And when a man has power to enjoy this allowance in comfort, it is God that <i>answereth him in the joy of his heart</i>, ver. 20. It is plain then that God <i>sows good <i>seed</i> in his field</i>; the springing up therefore of these tares of vexation, which so generally afflict the sons of men, must be ascribed to this: <i>the enemy hath done it.</i>
