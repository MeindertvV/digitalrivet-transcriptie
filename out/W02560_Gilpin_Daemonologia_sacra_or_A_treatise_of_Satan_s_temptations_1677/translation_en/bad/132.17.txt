[1] 2. <i>Wrath killeth the foolish man, and envy slayeth the foolish one.</i> This is not to be understood only of its <i>provoking</i> the Judge of all the earth to send down the destruction it deserves, but also (if not chiefly) of its own <i>corroding</i> temper, which, by long continuance, wastes the strength and consumes the body. Pride is a <i>perpetual vexation</i>, creating its troubles out of its own fancy. Irregular covetings keep a man always on the rack; they make him like the Tantalus of the poets. They give him <i>a canine appetite</i>, a strong hunger with excessive greed, restless pursuit and <i>constant dissatisfactions</i>. He is ever <i>gaping</i> and never <i>enjoying</i>. Impatience is a wearisome conflict with a burden which it can neither <i>bear</i> nor <i>shake off</i>. All the fruit of this vain labour comes to no better account than this: the impatient man makes his burden <i>greater</i>, the bands that tie it on <i>stronger</i>, and the strength that should bear it <i>weaker</i>. Lastly, an excess of pity multiplies <i>wounds without cause</i>. It hinders a man from being <i>happy</i> so long as there are any who are <i>miserable</i>. He is always, as far as his quiet goes, at the mercy of other men; the afflicted can torment him at a distance and, by a kind of <i>Magick</i>, make him feel the torments inflicted on his image. Who can deny that men ridden by such vexatious dispositions must lead an unquiet life and always be tossed by inward tempests? Especially (2) when we consider <i>how fit</i> the aforementioned <i>occasions</i> are to draw these <i>humours</i> out into their tumultuous extravagances. A lighted match and gunpowder are not more exactly suited to raise a shattering blast than those occasions and tempers are to breed inward annoyance. Some of these humours are so troublesome that, rather than go without work, they will fight with their <i>own shadows</i>, and by a perverse <i>prejudiced fancy</i> will create their own troubles. The best of them, which sometimes seem to make a truce and settle into rest while occasions are out of the way, are <i>quickly awakened</i>, like sleeping dogs roused by the least noise. What work, then, may we expect them to make when they are summoned to appear on a solemn occasion? But (3) if we deal by instances and bring onto the stage the effects that these concurring causes have produced, it will appear that they cause disturbances in earnest. Let us look either at the <i>furious fits</i>, which, like sudden flashes, are soonest gone, or at their more <i>lasting impressions</i>, and we shall find it true. As to violent fits raised by such occasions and dispositions, examples are endless. What rages, outrages, madnesses and extravagances have men run into? Some, provoked, have furiously committed <i>savage cruelties</i>, and for small matters have been carried to the most desperate revenges. Others have been brought to such violent <i>commotions</i> within themselves that the frame of nature has been thereby <i>weakened</i> and overthrown. Such was Scilla, who, in a strong passion, vomited up choler until he died. Some, in their fury, have done what has been a matter of <i>sorrow</i> to them all their days. But (setting aside the examples of heathens and wicked men) let us consider the wonderful <i>transports</i> of holy men. Moses, a man eminent beyond comparison in meekness, was so astonished by a sudden surprise of trouble at the sight of the golden calf that he threw down the tables of the law and broke them. Some indeed observe from this a <i>significancy</i> in <i>Israel's</i> breaking the law and forfeiting God's protection as his peculiar people. But <i>this</i> is to be ascribed more to the <i>designment</i> of <i>Divine Providence</i>, which so ordered it, than to the <i>intendment</i> of Moses, who, no doubt, did not do this from a <i>sedate</i> and calm <i>deliberation</i>, as purposing by this act to tell <i>Israel</i> so much, but was hurried by his grief, not considering well what he did, to break them. Asa, a good man, when he was reproved by the prophet, instead of thankfully accepting the rebuke, grew angry, fell into a rage, and threw the prophet into prison. Elijah, discomposed by Jezebel's persecution, asked that God would take away his life. Jonah, in his anger, fell out with God and <i>justifies</i> it when he had done so. Surely such fits as these proceeded from great inward combustion. Would wise, sober, holy men have said or done such things if they had not been transported <i>beyond</i> themselves? And though in such cases the fits are soon over, we observe that some are apt to fall into such fits <i>often</i>, and are so <i>easily</i> irritated that (like the epileptic person possessed by the devil) upon every occasion they are cast by him <i>into the fire or into the water</i>, and by the <i>frequent return</i> of their distemper are <i>never</i> at rest.
