[1] 1. It is a truth that <i>God</i> sometimes <i>wounds</i> the consciences of his children, and this happens, first, <i>before</i> conversion. In that state men are still in their sins, walking in the vanity of their minds. To <i>translate</i> them out of this condition, he awakens the conscience, shows them their iniquities and the danger of them, that for the present they are <i>in their blood</i>, <i>children of wrath</i>, <i>as well as others</i>, and that without <i>Christ</i> they are miserable. The effect of this must needs be <i>serious consideration</i>, deep thoughts of the heart, with some <i>trouble</i>. Only in the <i>measure</i> and degree there is great <i>difference</i>. In applying these things to the conscience, God does not tie himself exactly to the same <i>manner</i> and <i>measure</i> of proceeding, though he keeps to his <i>general method</i>. Hence it is that some, in regard to God's gentle and unhurried dealing and the frequent interposition of encouragements, are (compared with the case of others) said to be <i>allured</i> and <i>drawn with cords of love</i>. But others have a remarkable measure of trouble, sharp fits of fear and anguish. Those most commonly are such whose conversion is more <i>quick</i>, and the change visible from one <i>extreme</i> to <i>another</i> (as Paul's was, when he was converted in the midst of his raging persecution), or those whose ignorance or melancholy makes their hopes and comforts inaccessible for the present. God owns these troubles as the work of his Spirit. The same Spirit who is a <i>Spirit of adoption</i> to the converted is a <i>Spirit of bondage</i> to these, <i>Rom. 8. 16.</i> Accordingly we find that this was so with the converts in <i>Acts</i> 2, who, being <i>pricked in their hearts</i> by Peter's sermon, <i>cried out</i>, <i>Men and brethren, what shall we do?</i> The jailer did the same. And the promise God makes of calling the <i>Jews, Zach. 12. 10.</i> expresses God's purpose of dealing with them in this very method: <i>They shall look upon him whom they have pierced, and shall mourn for him as one that mourneth for his only son, and shall be in bitterness for him; in that day shall be a great mourning.</i> 2. God also sometimes wounds the conscience of his children <i>after conversion</i>, and he does this to convince and humble them for some failing into which they have fallen: when they grow <i>secure</i>, carnally confident of the continuance of their peace; when they are carelessly <i>negligent</i> of duty and of the exercise of their graces; when they fall into <i>gross</i> and scandalous sins, or wilfully <i>desert</i> the ways of truth; and in many other cases of the same kind. When his children make themselves thus liable to divine displeasure, God hides his face from them, takes away his Spirit, signifies his anger to their consciences, and threatens them with the danger of that condition. From this follow grief and fear in the hearts of his people. In this manner God expressed his displeasure to <i>David</i>, as his complaints in <i>Psal. 51.</i> testify: <i>Make me to hear joy and gladness, that the bones which thou hast broken may rejoice; hide thy face from my sins. Cast me not away from thy presence, and take not thy holy Spirit from me. Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation, &c.</i>
