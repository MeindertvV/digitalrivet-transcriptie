[1] Thirdly, where the aforementioned particulars of inclination, natural or acquired humours, custom, education, and so on, neither <i>divert</i> the understanding nor <i>engage</i> it to embrace error, yet they often reveal how powerfully they can bias the mind, in that these prevail with men to <i>modify and mould a truth according to the bent, or form, of their inclinations</i>. As a bowl skilfully aimed at a <i>mark</i> nevertheless travels by a <i>curve</i> to which its bias drives it, according to the rises or falls of the ground it meets on the way, so men may arrive at real truth in the main, and yet <i>shape</i> it according to their <i>humours</i>. For instance, let us consider the different <i>modes</i> or forms in which the <i>same truth</i> is represented under the workings of <i>different</i> tempers. A <i>melancholy</i> person conceives of all things under such <i>reflections</i> as fear and sadness usually give: if he considers God, he looks upon him under the notion of <i>greatest severity and justice</i>; if upon the ways of <i>duty</i>, he colours them all <i>in black</i>, and can scarcely account anything <i>piety</i> which is not accompanied with <i>sadness</i> and <i>mourning</i>; if he calls his <i>soul</i> to a reckoning, his conclusions concerning himself are <i>sad</i>, doleful, or at best <i>suspicious</i>. On the contrary, a <i>cheerful</i> and <i>hilarious</i> temper counts all <i>sadness</i> as <i>sullenness</i>, and is apt to accuse those who go <i>mourning</i> on their way of being <i>ungrateful murmurers</i> and <i>unbelieving complainers</i>; it interprets God's favourable <i>condescensions</i> to the weak in the widest <i>latitude</i>, and is easily persuaded of those things that lie on the <i>utmost brink</i> of liberty, which others of a <i>more fearful</i> disposition dare not <i>approach</i>, for fear of offending.
