[1] The imperfection of our knowledge, being thus laid open, it is easy to see what advantages the Devil may make of it for promoting error; for it must now become our wonder, not that <i>any man errs</i>, but that <i>all do not</i>. We find it easy to impose anything upon children; it is an easy matter for a trifle to cheat them out of all they have. Surely then Satan may do as much with men, who are but <i>children in understanding</i>. The Apostle, <i>Ephes.</i> 4. 14. puts us in mind of this hazard under that very similitude: <i>That we henceforth be no more children tossed to and fro, and carried about with every wind of doctrine.</i> How fitly does he liken us to children! Their weaknesses are these: (1) want of <i>discernment</i>, for they do not see the true worth of things; (2) <i>credulity</i>, for they believe all fair speeches and specious promises. The hazard of both is that it makes them <i>unsteady, uncertain</i>, and <i>fickle</i>; and such are we made by our ignorance. So little do we truly discern, and so apt are we to <i>believe</i> every pretence (for <i>the simple believeth every word</i>, Prov. 14. 15.), that, as the Apostle's metaphors tell us, we are easily <i>tossed</i> from one conceit or opinion to another, as a <i>ship</i> is by the waves, or a <i>feather</i> by the wind.
