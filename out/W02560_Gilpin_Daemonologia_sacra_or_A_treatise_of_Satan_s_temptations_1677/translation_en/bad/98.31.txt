[1] Whoever impartially compares the rites, customs, usages and garb of <i>Popery</i> with those of <i>Paganism</i> will, to his amazement, find such an exact <i>agreement</i> and consonance that he must conclude either that <i>Paganism</i> imitated <i>Popery</i>, or that <i>Popery</i> imitated <i>Paganism</i>. The latter is the truth, and that these corruptions came into religion through <i>Popery</i> by <i>a design of conforming Christianity to heathenism</i> (though at first it may have been from <i>pious intentions</i>) is not difficult to prove. For, besides the fact that the rites of <i>Paganism</i> were more <i>ancient</i> and so could not have been borrowed from Popery, which came <i>long after</i>, Scripture foretold a great <i>defection</i> from the truth, which would occur in the <i>last days</i>, and this <i>under a profession</i> of religion. The things <i>particularized</i> show that the defection would carry an <i>imitation</i> of Paganism. No less seems to be signified by 1 Tim. 4. 1: "The Spirit speaketh expressly, that in the latter times some shall depart from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits, and doctrines of devils." That is, as <i>Mr. Mede</i> (whose interpretation I follow) proves, the doctrines concerning devils or Dæmons (as in Heb. 6. 2 we have the phrase "doctrines of baptisms", which must signify doctrines concerning baptisms): the Gentile <i>theology of Dæmons</i> is the thing which Paul prophesies would be introduced into Christianity. How clearly this relates to <i>Popery</i> may be evident to anyone who does not wilfully blind himself by prejudice. Their doctrine of <i>Dæmons</i> was this: they supposed <i>two sorts</i> of gods, <i>supreme</i> and <i>inferior</i>. The supreme they supposed dwelt in the <i>heavenly lights</i>, the sun, moon and stars, without <i>change</i> of place; these they judged to be so <i>sublime</i> and pure that they might not be profaned by the approach of earthly things, and that <i>immediate</i> approaches to them were <i>derogatory</i> to their sovereignty. The inferior order of gods they imagined to be of a <i>middle</i> sort, between the <i>supreme</i> beings and men, as partaking of <i>both</i>. These they called <i>mediators</i> and agents, and supposed their business was to <i>carry up men's prayers to God</i> and to <i>bring down blessings</i> from God upon men. In Scripture these were called <i>Baalim</i>, and by the Greeks <i>Dæmons</i>. To this purpose Austin and others speak.
