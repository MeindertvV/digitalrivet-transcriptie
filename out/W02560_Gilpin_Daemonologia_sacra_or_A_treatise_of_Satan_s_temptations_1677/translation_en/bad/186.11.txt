[1] 2. Besides, the <i>impressions</i> of melancholy are always <i>strong</i>. It is <i>strong in its fears</i> (or else men would never be tempted to de<i>stroy</i> themselves). It is strong in its <i>mistakes</i> (or else they could never persuade themselves of the truth of <i>foolish</i>, <i>absurd</i>, and <i>impossible</i> fancies), as in the case of <i>Nebuchadnezzar</i>, who, by a delusive apprehension, believed himself to be <i>a Beast</i>, forsook the company of men, and betook himself to the fields to eat grass with the oxen. The imaginations of the melancholic are <i>never idle</i>, and yet they are <i>straightened</i>, or confined to a few things. Then, the brain being weakened as to a true and <i>regular</i> apprehension, it frames nothing but bugbears, and yet with the highest confidence of certainty. 3. These impressions are usually <i>lasting</i>. They do not vanish like an <i>early dew</i>, but continue for months and years. 4. And yet they have only <i>so much</i> understanding left them as serves to nourish their fears. If their understanding had been <i>quite</i> gone, their fears would vanish with it, as a flame is extinguished for want of air. But they have only <i>knowledge</i> enough to let them <i>see</i> their misery, and <i>sense</i> enough to make them <i>apprehensive</i> of their pain. Therefore they will <i>pray</i> with floods of <i>tears</i>, inexpressible <i>groanings</i>, deepest <i>sighing</i>, and <i>trembling</i> joints, to be <i>delivered</i> from their fears. 5. They are also apt, after relief from their troubles, to suffer <i>frequent returns</i>. What disposition, all these things being considered, can be more exactly shaped to serve Satan's turn? If he would have men believe <i>the worst</i> of themselves, he has such imaginations to work upon, already misshapen into a <i>deformity</i> of evil surmising. Would he <i>terrify</i> by fears, or <i>distress</i> by sadness? He has that already; and it needs only <i>altering the object</i> (which often is not even needed, for the serious melancholic naturally spends all his griefs on his supposed miserable estate of soul), and then he has spiritual distress. Would he keep them <i>long</i> under their sorrows, or take them up on all occasions <i>at his pleasure</i>, or act them out to a <i>greater height</i> than usual? The melancholic temper still suits him. This is enough to make us cautious: we must take special care of our bodies, by all lawful means, to prevent or abate that humour, if we would not have the devil abuse us at his will.
