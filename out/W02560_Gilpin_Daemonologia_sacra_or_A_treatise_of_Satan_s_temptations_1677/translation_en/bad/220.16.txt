[1] As to the first part of the question, I <i>answer</i>: first, that <i>not all</i> spiritual distresses are to be ascribed to melancholy. For, first, there are some melancholy persons who are never <i>more free</i> from spiritual troubles (though they are often troubled by them at other times) than when, on the occasion of some special affliction or sickness threatening death, there is <i>greatest cause</i> to fear such attacks, which would come with an <i>increase</i> of melancholy. I have known some such people. Second, these distresses sometimes come <i>suddenly</i>, their conscience striking them in the very <i>act</i> of sin, and sometimes they come upon persons who are not of a <i>melancholick constitution</i>. <i>Spira</i> was struck suddenly with terrors of conscience when he recanted some truths he had held, and so were some of the martyrs. Sometimes terrors that have lasted <i>long</i> and been very <i>fierce</i> are removed in a <i>moment</i>. Now, it is not reasonable to say that melancholy alone caused all such troubles, when the bodies in question are not <i>naturally</i> of that complexion (and some such people have been seized by terrors of conscience). If we take the liberty of supposing an <i>accidental melancholy</i>, we must necessarily allow <i>some</i> time for it, and usually some <i>preceding occasion</i> to shape them into such a disorder. Nor do the <i>fears</i> of melancholy cease <i>suddenly</i>, but abate <i>gradually</i>, in step with the gradual lessening of the humour. To say that the despair of <i>Cain</i> or of <i>Judas</i> was the assault of strong melancholy is not only beyond all <i>proof</i>, but also beyond all <i>probability</i>. Nor is it likely that <i>David</i> (whose <i>ruddy</i> complexion and inclination to <i>music</i> are signs of a sanguine temperament) was <i>always</i> melancholy under his frequent complaints of spiritual trouble.
