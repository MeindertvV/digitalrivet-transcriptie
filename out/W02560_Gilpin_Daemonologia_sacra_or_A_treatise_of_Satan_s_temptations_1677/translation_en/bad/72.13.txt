[1] Thirdly, where the two former means fail, men who devote themselves to this kind of service imitate their father the Devil and resort to plain <i>lying</i> and <i>invented fables</i>. <i>Idolatry</i> was chiefly propped up by <i>fabulous</i> stories; and no wonder, when they judged it a pious fraud to nourish devotion towards the gods, in which case (as Polybius says) although their writers relate <i>monsters</i> and write childish, absurd and impossible things, yet they are to be pardoned <i>for their good intent</i>. Among the Papists what less can be expected, when the same principle is held among them? Canus and Ludovicus Vives, whom he mentions, (as also some few others) greatly blame that blind piety of <i>coining lies</i> for religion and feigning histories to support their opinions; but while they with great frankness and candour <i>censure</i> the fables of their own party, they plainly acknowledge that they are too much <i>guilty</i> of feigning, so much so that not only the author of the Golden Legend is branded with the marks of a Brazen Face and a Leaden Heart, but also Gregory's Dialogues and Bede's History are blamed by him as containing narrations of <i>miracles taken upon trust</i> from the reports of the common people. And indeed the wonders they talk of are so <i>strange</i>, so <i>unlikely</i>, so <i>ridiculous</i>, and some of them so <i>absurd</i>, that unless a man does violence to his reason and wilfully shuts his eyes against the clear grounds of <i>suspicion</i>, he cannot think they are anything else than dreams and fables, no better than <i>Aesop's</i>; you may meet with several catalogues of them in Protestant writers. Such as St. Swithin making a whole basket of broken eggs whole again by the sign of the cross; Patrick making the stolen sheep bleat in the thief's belly after he had eaten it; St. Bridget's bacon, which in great charity she gave to a hungry dog, being found again in her kettle; Dionysius, after he was beheaded, carrying his head in his hand three French miles. St. Dunstan took the Devil by the nose with his tongs till he made him roar. Dominic made him hold the candle till he burnt his fingers. St. Lupus imprisoned the Devil in a pot all night. A chapel of the Virgin Mary was translated from Palestine to Loreto. A consecrated Host (being put into a hive of bees to cure them of the murrain) was so devoutly entertained that the bees built a chapel in the hive, with doors, windows, steeple and bells, erected an altar, laid the Host upon it, sang their canonical hours, and kept their watches by night as monks used to do in their cloisters, etc. Who would ever imagine that men of any <i>seriousness</i> could satisfy themselves with such <i>childish fopperies</i>? These are the usual ways by which men of design have raised the <i>noise</i> of miracles.
