[1] The lies that are fit to splatter a <i>Truth</i> are those that tend to make it seem <i>unlovely</i>, <i>inconvenient</i>, or <i>dangerous</i>. Satan has never been slow to raise mists and fogs to eclipse the shining beauty of Truth. Sometimes he persuades men that it is a <i>novelty</i>, contrary <i>to the tradition of the Fathers</i>. Then, if an error has once appeared on the stage and been hissed out of the world again, when it peeps out once more its former impudence is made an argument for its antiquity, and Truth is decried as new. Or, if it is only an error of yesterday that has gained an age or two, then the <i>ghosts</i> of our forefathers are conjured up as witnesses, and the plea runs smoothly: <i>What has become of your fathers? Or are you wiser than your fathers? Are they all damned</i> [?] These were pressed by the <i>Heathens</i>: they thought the gods of the country and the worship of their fathers should not be forsaken for Christianity, which they judged to be only a <i>novelty</i> compared with <i>Paganism</i>. Of the same stamp is that <i>old song</i> of the Papists, <i>Where was your religion before Luther?</i> To this end they also talk of the succession of their <i>bishops</i> and <i>popes</i>. Other errors grow a little <i>pert</i> and confident if they can find a <i>pattern</i> or sample for themselves among the <i>old heresies</i>. Sometimes he endeavours to bring Truth into suspicion by representing it as a dangerous <i>encroachment</i> upon the <i>rights</i> and privileges of men, as if it would turn everything upside down and introduce factions and confusions. This clamour was raised against the Gospel, that it would <i>subvert</i> <i>Moses and the</i> Law. Sometimes he dresses the opinions of Truth in an <i>ugly dress</i>, and misrepresents it to the world as guilty of <i>strange</i> inferences and <i>absurdities</i>, which arise only from a <i>wrong stating</i> of the questions. And where it does <i>really</i> differ from error, he endeavours to <i>widen</i> the differences to an inconvenient distance, so that if Truth goes <i>a mile</i> from error, Satan will have it <i>go two</i>. If Truth teaches <i>justification by faith</i>, error represents it as <i>denying</i> all care of <i>holiness</i> and <i>good works</i>. If Truth says that <i>bare moral virtues</i> are not sufficient <i>without grace</i>, error at once accuses it of denying any <i>necessary use of morality</i>, or of affirming that <i>moral</i> virtues are <i>obstructions</i> and hindrances to salvation. It would be easy to note abundance of such instances.
