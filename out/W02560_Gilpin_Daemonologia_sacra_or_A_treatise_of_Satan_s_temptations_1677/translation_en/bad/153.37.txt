[1] 7. These are very <i>grievous burdens</i>, and hard to bear for many reasons. (1.) Who can fully express the inward <i>torture</i> and disturbance of the mind when it is forced, against its own <i>natural bent</i> and inclination, to harbour such monsters within itself? How would nature recoil and abhor being made to drink down foul muddy water, or to swallow toads and serpents? (This is why persecutors in their devilish schemes invented such tortures.) And what less does the Devil do when he forces blasphemies upon their thoughts and <i>commits a rape</i> on their imaginations by malicious violence? David, under these temptations, cries out in Psal. 73. 21: <i>Thus my heart was grieved, and I was pricked in my reins.</i> It cannot be otherwise, for the reason already mentioned. Nature abhors being forced into what is most contrary to itself, and so does grace. Now the things by which Satan works these frights are contrary to nature or to grace, or to both; and as they strive with all their strength to cast out what is so opposed to them, so the Devil, if he would carry out his design, must exert all his power to strengthen his assault. From this, as when fire and water are brought together, arises a most troublesome conflict. Indeed, if there were a <i>compliance</i> of our consent, there would be <i>no affrightment</i>; nor can this kind of temptation be carried on unless there is the <i>utmost dissent</i> of the mind. If anyone thinks there is no great <i>ground</i> for these temptations, because some of the things by which he is said to frighten men are <i>natural</i> to us, such as <i>atheistical</i> thoughts (which some call the <i>master-vein</i> of our original corruption, and others say are in the heart of <i>every</i> man by nature), and consequently not as troublesome as is imagined, I answer: when divines call these thoughts, or blasphemous thoughts, natural, they do not mean that they are <i>natural impressions engraven on us by creation</i> (for they assert the contrary). They mean that there is a natural and <i>unextinguishable</i> impression on every man that <i>there is a God</i>, and they usually give this as proof that the greatest atheists, in <i>fear</i> and extremity, show a <i>secret belief</i> in a Deity by calling out <i>O God</i>, or by some other <i>posture</i>, as Caligula did when he hid himself at the thunder. But they mean only that our <i>natural corruption may produce these thoughts</i>, and that they are its natural issues. Therefore Perkins, answering a question of this kind, tells us that these two thoughts, <i>[there is a God]</i> and <i>[there is no God]</i>, may both be, and are, <i>in the same</i> heart. Now this gives us the <i>reason</i> why Satan chooses these thoughts to trouble us with, which may also rise from ourselves (as I have hinted before and shall touch on again presently). It also tells us that whether these thoughts arise from our own corruption or from Satan, our <i>natural impressions</i> are strong against them, and that they cannot be so frightful unless Satan manages them, and unless the contrary impressions of nature are awakened to resist strongly. Then that struggle must be like the tearing of our bowels, and it is all the worse because we are incessantly pursued: Satan, with tireless labour, keeps casting back the same thoughts as they are repulsed and rejected, just as soldiers besieging cities cast fired grenades over the walls. (2.) These also are grievous, for they put the mind on the <i>rack</i> and stretch it through laborious and <i>doubtful enquiries</i> after the grounds or <i>causes</i> of this kind of trouble. For the heart, astonished at such cursed guests against its will, at once reflects on God and on itself: <i>What have I done?</i> and why am I thus disturbed by monsters? Why does the righteous Lord allow Satan to break open my heart and fill me with such fearful thoughts? But when men's enquiries do not rise higher, and they stay occupied with considering the <i>nature</i> of the trouble and the <i>manner</i> of its working, without looking up to the providence of God, then their troubles are <i>increased</i>. (3.) As these injections force men to defend themselves, to <i>oppose</i> and resist in every way, this is another increase of the burden. What pleadings they are put to! What defiances! What efforts to turn away the thoughts! And all to little purpose: while the trouble lasts, they are forced to lie in their armour and to stay constantly on guard. (4.) And they are troublesome in yet another way, in the <i>after-game</i> that Satan plays with these thoughts. It is not his whole design to frighten men; he usually has another temptation following close behind this one, which is to turn these <i>affrightments</i> into <i>accusations</i>. By urging them <i>long</i> upon men's hearts, he makes them believe that they are their <i>own</i> thoughts, the issues of their own natural corruption. Once men are weakened by continual assaults, their senses and memory dulled, their understanding confused, and so on, they easily conclude against themselves. The Tempter imputes all the horrid blasphemy to them, boldly calls them guilty of everything, and because their thoughts have dwelt long on such a <i>subject</i>, and because they know that corrupt nature of <i>itself</i> will lead men to such horrid blasphemies or villainies (which makes it <i>probable</i> that it might be their own fault, and for this <i>reason</i> Satan chooses such injections as may seem most <i>likely</i> to be true in the accusation), they, being strongly charged as guilty, <i>yield</i>. Then begins another trouble, more fearful than the former. Oh! What sad thoughts they then have of themselves, as the most vile and blasphemous wretches! Sometimes they think <i>that it is impossible that other men's hearts should harbour such intolerable things as theirs</i>, and <i>that none was ever as bad as they</i>. Sometimes they think <i>that if men knew what vile imaginations and monstrous things are in their minds, they would, in very zeal for God and religion, stone them, or at least exclude them from all dealings with men</i>. Sometimes they think <i>their sin to be the sin against the Holy Ghost</i>. Sometimes they think <i>God is engaged, in point of honour, to show some remarkable judgment upon them</i>, and they truly look for some terrible stroke to crush them, and live under such a frightful expectation. These and many more thoughts of this kind trouble them, so that these temptations are troublesome in every way, both in their first and in their second effects.
