[1] 1. Some are desperate terrors of <i>cursed reprobates</i>, under despair. These terrors are at their greatest extremity, the very pit of misery, and of the same nature as those of the damned in hell, <i>where the worm that never dies</i> is nothing else but the dreadful vexation and torment of an accusing conscience. They are commonly accompanied by <i>blaspheming</i> God and an utter <i>rejection</i> of all means of remedy. Though they sometimes turn into a kind of <i>secure desperation</i>, (when people see that things will get no better, they <i>harden</i> themselves in their misery and seek to <i>divert</i> their thoughts), as Cain did, when he turned to the building of cities. And Esau, when he had sold his birthright, despised it and gave himself up to the pursuit of worldly advantage. Yet sometimes these terrors end in <i>self-murder</i>, as in Judas, who, struck with dread of conscience, went and hanged himself. We have many sad instances of these desperate terrors. Cain is the first we read of, and though the account the Scriptures give of him is short, it is enough to let us see what his condition was (Gen. 4. 11 to 16). First, he was <i>cursed from the earth</i>. This part of his curse had two branches. (1) That his labour and toil in tillage should be <i>great</i>, and <i>greatly</i> unsuccessful; for God himself explains it thus, ver. 12: <i>When thou tillest the ground, it shall not henceforth yield unto thee her strength.</i> The earth was cursed with barrenness before, for Adam, but now for Cain it bears a <i>double</i> curse. (2) That he should be a man of uncertain abode in any place: <i>A fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth</i>, not able to stay long in one place because of the terrors of his conscience. His own interpretation of this, ver. 14, shows that a great part of his misery lay here: <i>Thou hast driven me out this day from the face of the earth.</i> By this it appears that he was to be as one chased out of all society, and as one who thought himself safe in no place. Secondly, he was <i>hid from the face of God</i>; that is, he was doomed to carry the inward feeling of God's wrath, without any expectation of mercy. Thirdly, his mind being terrified under the apprehension of that wrath, he cries out that his <i>sin was greater than it could be pardoned</i>, or that <i>his punishment was greater than he could bear.</i> (For the word in the original signifies both sin and punishment.) Take it whichever way you will, it expresses a deep horror of heart. If the former sense is meant, it signifies a conviction of the greatness of his sin, amounting to despair; if the latter, it is no less than a blasphemous reflection upon God as unjustly cruel. Fourthly, this horror was so great that he was afraid of all he met, suspecting everything to be armed with divine vengeance against him: <i>every one that findeth me, shall slay me.</i> Or if that speech was a wish that anyone who found him might kill him (as some interpret it), it shows that he preferred death to that life of misery. It seems, then, that God struck him with such terror and consternation of mind, and with such frightful trembling of body, for his bloody deed, that he was weary of himself, afraid of all men, and could not stay long in one place. By these tokens (or some other way) God set his <i>mark</i> upon him, as upon a cursed wretch, to be noted and abhorred by all. Another such instance was Lamech, of whom the same chapter speaks.
