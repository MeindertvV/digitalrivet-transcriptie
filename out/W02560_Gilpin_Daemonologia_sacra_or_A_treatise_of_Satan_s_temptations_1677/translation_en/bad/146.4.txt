[1] If Satan's power were bounded here, and he could do no more than set before men occasions of vexation, we might still justly call him the troubler of the spirits of men on that single account, considering that the thoughts of men are naturally restless and their imaginations ever rolling. If men sequester themselves from all business, shut themselves up from commerce with men, and turn <i>hermits</i> (as Jerome did) on purpose to avoid disquiet, their thoughts would still <i>hurry</i> them from place to place: sometimes to the <i>court</i>, sometimes to the <i>market</i>, sometimes to <i>shows</i> and pastimes, sometimes to <i>quarrels</i>; sometimes they <i>view</i> fields, buildings and countries, sometimes they <i>fancy</i> dignities, promotions and honours. They are ever working upon one object or another, real or supposed, and according to the object, so the affections will be, high or low, joyful or sorrowful. So that if the utmost Satan could do were no more than to provide occasions, discomposures would follow naturally. The evil dispositions of men would thereby be set working, even though Satan stood by as an <i>idle spectator</i>. The serpent (in our breasts, as Solomon tells us, Eccles. 10. 11.) would <i>bite without enchantment</i>, that is, unless it were charmed.
