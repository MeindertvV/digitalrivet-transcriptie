[1] 1. These take away all alacrity and forwardness of mind, partly by diverting it from duty. Sorrows, when they prevail, so fix the mind upon the present trouble that it can think of nothing but its burden; they confine the thoughts to the pain and smart and make a man forget all other things, as David in his trouble forgot to eat his bread; and sick persons willingly talk of nothing but their diseases. Partly they do this by indisposing us for action. <i>Joy</i> and <i>hope</i> are <i>active</i> principles, but <i>sorrow</i> is <i>sullen</i> and sluggish. As the mind in trouble is wholly employed in contemplating its misery, rather than in <i>finding out a way</i> to escape it, so if at any time it has leisure to entertain thoughts of using means for recovery, yet it is so tired out with its burden, so disheartened by its own fears, so discouraged by opposition and disappointment, that it has no inclination to undertake anything. By this means the Devil brings the soul into a spiritual <i>catalepsy</i>, <i>congealing</i> the spirits so that it is made stiff and deprived of motion.
