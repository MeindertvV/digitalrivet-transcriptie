[1] I have often considered what reason might be given for the <i>obscurity</i> of such expressions, and I have been forced to satisfy myself with these. First, many mistake the knowledge of <i>words</i> for the knowledge of <i>things</i>. And poor ignorant men may well believe they have attained, no one knows what, by this device; when among learned men the knowledge of <i>words</i> is esteemed so great a height of learning, and they foster a great many controversies that are only <i>verbal</i>. Secondly, some are pleased to be accounted <i>understanders</i> by others, and rest in such high words as a <i>badge</i> of knowledge. Thirdly, some are delighted with such a hard language in the hope that it will lead them at last to the knowledge of the <i>things</i>: they think <i>strange expressions</i> are a sign of <i>deep</i> mysteries. I knew one who set himself to the reading of <i>Jacob Behmen's</i> books (though at present he confessed he was scarcely able to make common sense of three lines together), drawn by a secret enticement he felt from the language, to come by much pains and reading to some <i>excellent</i> discovery. Fourthly, some whose fancies are <i>heated</i> have by this means broken <i>confused impressions</i> of strange things in their imaginations, and conceive themselves to know things beyond what common language can express: as if with <i>Paul</i>, caught up into the third heaven, <i>they hear and see unutterable wonders.</i> But whatever reason prevails with men to take up such a way of speaking, Satan makes them believe that it contains a rich <i>mine</i> or treasury, not of common truths, but of extraordinary <i>profundities.</i>
