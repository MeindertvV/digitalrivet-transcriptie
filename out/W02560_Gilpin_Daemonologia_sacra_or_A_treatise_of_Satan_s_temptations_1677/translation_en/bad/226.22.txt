Where Satan does not have these particular advantages, he endeavours to prepare men for distresses by other troubles, <i>long continued.</i> Not all men who are brought to despair of their happiness must be supposed to be <i>greater sinners</i> than others. Some are distressed by fears of eternal damnation who are, to a good degree, able to make Job's protestation in these cases: that <i>their heart has not been deceived</i> by a woman; that they <i>have not laid wait at their neighbour's door</i>; that they have not <i>lifted up their hand against the fatherless, when they saw their help in the gate</i>; <i>that their land does not cry out against them, nor the furrows thereof complain</i>; <i>that when they saw the sun when it shined, or the moon walking in brightness, their heart has not been secretly enticed, nor their mouth kissed their hand</i>; <i>that they did not rejoice at the destruction of him that hated them, nor lift themselves up when evil found him,</i> etc. Notwithstanding all this, their fears are upon them and prevail against them. But before Satan can bring them to consent to such dismal conclusions against themselves, they must be <i>extraordinarily fitted</i> to receive the impression: either <i>worn out</i> under <i>great afflictions</i>, or <i>long</i> exercised with <i>fears</i> about their spiritual estates, without any mixture of comfort or ease, or their faculties <i>broken</i> and weakened by melancholy. Any of these gives him an advantage <i>equivalent</i> to that of <i>great sins.</i> For though he cannot say to these people that their sins are so enormous that they (considered by themselves together with their circumstances) are <i>sad signs</i> of reprobation, yet he will plead that <i>God's dealings</i> towards them plainly show that he has wholly cast them off and left them to themselves, without hope of mercy.
