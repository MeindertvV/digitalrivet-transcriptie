[1] 3. From some <i>remarkable appearances</i> of God, Satan aggravates our sinful condition. If God shows any notable act of <i>power</i>, he makes the beams of that act <i>reflect</i> upon our <i>unworthiness</i> with a dazzling light. When <i>Peter</i> saw the power of Christ in sending a great multitude of fishes into his net (having laboured all night before and caught nothing), it made such a deep impression of his vileness upon him that he was ready to put Christ from him as altogether unfit for his blessed society: <i>Depart (saith he) from me, for I am a sinful man.</i> If God discovers the glorious splendour of his <i>holiness</i>, it is enough to make the holiest saints (such as <i>Job</i> and <i>Isaiah</i>) cry out that they are <i>undone</i>, being <i>men of unclean lips</i>, and to <i>abhor themselves in dust and ashes</i>. The same may be said of any discovery of the rest of the glorious attributes of God. Of all this Satan makes this advantage: that the parties tempted should consider their unworthiness so deeply that they might come to believe (as if by a voice from heaven) that God <i>forbids</i> them any approach to him, and that they have no business taking God's name into their mouths. And although these remarkable discoveries of God (whether by his acts of power and providence, or by immediate impressions upon the soul in the height of contemplation) ordinarily have <i>great effects</i> upon the hearts of his children, though not of long continuance, yet where they strike in with <i>other</i> arguments by which they were <i>already</i> shaken as to their interest in God, they greatly strengthen them, and are taken for no less than God's own determination of the question against them.
