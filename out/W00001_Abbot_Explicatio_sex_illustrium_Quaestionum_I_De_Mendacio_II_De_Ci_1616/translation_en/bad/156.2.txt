[1] 16 This is also the most pious and most solid judgment of Augustine on Athanasius' withdrawal, and on others as well, in his letter to Honoratus, which anyone who wishes to know more about these matters should consult, if any other. There the most learned Father, deliberately treating the question now before us, asserts that the necessary ministry of the Church must by no means be abandoned, on pain of danger to the soul. "Far be it," he says, "that our ship should be held in such low esteem, that is, that the Church should be regarded so cheaply, that the sailors, and especially the helmsman, should desert it in peril, even if they could escape by leaping into a skiff or even by swimming." He also sets before us the wretchedness of a congregation thus abandoned and left widowed, and by the name of the hireling, and of that vengeance which belongs to the hireling, he deters the minister from fleeing. In a personal persecution, such as he calls that of Athanasius, he affirms that it is lawful to turn one's back, since others are present to take his place. But if the rage be general, against all the godly or against all ministers, prudence and fidelity must be used. If the people perish, the priest should also offer himself at the same time, as a leader to heaven; but if the whole people flee, so that no one remains who needs teaching, then the minister too may depart. But if there is peace among the laity, the priest should take care how he withdraws, since they too must not be left without a leader, and among them there will not be lacking hiding places where he may lie concealed under God's promise. But if, while the tyrant rages against the ministers alone, several and numerous ministers are found in the same flock, he judges that, if they have agreed among themselves, some should remain so that they may shine before the people, and the rest should depart so that they may be set aside as a seminary for the Church. Yet, lest anyone be compelled to stay against his will, or another, too presumptuous about his own strength, choose to remain, he thinks the matter should be decided by lot, or by casting lots. Nothing is more learned than Augustine; nothing more holy; nothing more prudent; and the Orthodox Church, so far as I know, has agreed with him from that time on. But we too add our vote, summing up in this conclusion all that has been said: that a lay man in persecution may flee, and likewise a woman or a young man, because they will not have to give an account for other people's souls; but that the minister, if he has pastoral care, must wait, provided that the persecution is not directed against his own person while there are several ministers; and that, along with the rest, he may hand over the common care to others for a time. And so much for this subject.
