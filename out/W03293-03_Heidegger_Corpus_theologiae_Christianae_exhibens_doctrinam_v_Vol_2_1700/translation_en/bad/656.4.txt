[1] <i>Rock</i> or <i>crag</i> is what he is to the Church, inasmuch as he, as the one Rock, bears all the faithful and all who are to be saved by his eternal promise <i>with powerful word</i>, Heb. 1.3. Nor would it follow, even if Peter had been marked by Christ with the name of rock, as some Fathers grant, that Christ declared him the Ecumenical Pontiff of the Church. Peter would bear the name of rock not properly but by a figurative use [πετρωνύμως], inasmuch as he was then a living stone of the spiritual building of the Church and a fellow-worker [συνεργὸς] in the work of Christ. St Peter himself explains his own name in this way in 1 Pet. 2.4-5, calling Christ the <i>living stone</i>, that is, the life-giving one, and those who come to him <i>living stones</i>, that is, made alive and built up on the life-giving Rock. Dead, he left a doctrine which was, is today, and will for ever be the firmest rock and foundation of the Church. Properly, however, the rock is either the faith and doctrine which Peter confessed, or Christ himself whom he confessed, who is named by the name of rock. For Christ is that <i>stone laid in Zion, precious, a cornerstone, most surely founded</i>, Isa. 28.16. Since Peter was the first of all Christ's disciples, and at the very beginning of the Gospel preaching most clearly confessed the mystery of Christ's incarnation and deity, Christ rightly recalled the minds of his disciples to those words of Isaiah, so that they might learn what that stone in Zion is, the foundation that is never shaken. <i>No one can lay another foundation than that which is laid, which is Jesus Christ</i>, 1 Cor. 3.11. <i>The rock was Christ</i>, ib. 10.4. <i>Building up yourselves on your most holy faith in the Holy Spirit</i>, Jude v. 20. And Scripture often marks God with the name of rock [צור]. Moreover, the distinction between <i>rock</i> and <i>foundation</i> as primary and secondary, absolute and vicarious, by which they assert that Christ was the primary and absolute rock and foundation, but Peter the secondary and vicarious one, flatly contradicts Scripture, according to which <i>no one can lay another [ἄλλο] foundation besides that which is laid</i>, namely Christ, 1 Cor. 3.11. Now a <i>secondary, vicarious</i> foundation is not merely <i>another [ἕτερον]</i> foundation, which it would be wrong even to lay, since no other was to be laid <i>outside [ἐκτὸς]</i> that which the Apostles laid. It is also a <i>different [ἄλλο]</i> foundation, numerically distinct, and indeed a foreign foundation of a private house and a private church, utterly different from the Church of Christ, and in no way of the kind that <i>stone laid in Zion, precious, a cornerstone, most surely founded</i> is. For no one can properly be the foundation of the Church except through the merit of the blood on which, as the one foundation, the Church is built. St Peter, however, did not win salvation and life for the Church by his blood. Nor does the Church need any foundation besides Christ, since he alone touches all the faithful by his Spirit, not through a vicar Peter or any other man, but through faith alone. Although we are built <i>upon the foundation of the Apostles and Prophets</i>, Eph. 2.20, and the names of the Apostles are said to be written on <i>twelve foundations</i> of the Church, Rev. 21.14, the mention there is not of one <i>foundation</i>, a vicarious and secondary one, namely Peter's, but of the foundation of the Apostles and of <i>twelve foundations</i>. Not that any of the Apostles was properly the foundation of the Church, but because they were the builders of that same foundation, placed there by the twelve Apostles as skilful architects and <i>fellow-workers of God</i>, bearing witness and demonstrating, 1 Cor. 3.9. In this sense, however, the passage Matt. 16.18 cannot be understood of St Peter, because it is not he but Christ, as chief architect, who is said to be going to build the Church upon that rock. For an architect laying the foundation cannot be called the <i>rock</i> or the <i>foundation</i> itself, except allegorically [ἀλληγορῶς]. Whether Christ spoke these words in Greek or in Syriac is uncertain, for such Grecisms were by no means infrequent among the people at that time. Yet even if he spoke in Syriac, it is by no means necessary that he used the word ܟܐܦܐ [Cepha] in both places, since he could speak thus: <i>You are Cephas</i> (as an adjective), and <i>I will build my Church upon this Cepha, the rock</i> (as a noun). For how could the disciples, or Peter himself, have understood Christ if he had kept the same word in both places, saying: <i>You are Rock; and upon this Rock I will build my Church?</i> Yet even if Christ had kept the same word in both places, the pronoun <i>this</i>, which the Syriac translator also has, would have distinguished the feminine later word from the earlier masculine one. Whatever is made of this, Matthew, who under the Divine Spirit distinguished these words as masculine and feminine, naming the thing and the name given, and who alone represents the authentic and canonical text, removes all doubt. For the pronoun need not refer to what precedes, since it can point out, and truly does point out, the person of Christ, comprised in the voice of Peter as a form in its subject, or a name in what is named. Vainly do they also object that future clause, <i>I will build my Church</i>, as though it could not be done otherwise than through Peter as a newly laid foundation, since Christ had already built the Apostles and disciples upon himself. For although Christ has always been the foundation of the Church, he was yet to build the Church, not with respect to Peter, but the Christian Church, that is, the spiritual house, built out of Jews and Gentiles through the death of Christ upon Christ as the one Rock, and fitted for the Kingdom of Heaven. And although Christ spoke those words to Peter alone, because in building the Church Peter would be almost the first spiritual architect laying the foundations above Christ, first among the Jews, in that incomparable sermon, Acts 2.14 ff., and then among the Gentiles, whose firstfruits he was, when he was moved by the heavenly vision in which the calling of the Gentiles and the liberty of the New Testament were explained to him, Acts 10.10 ff., and having converted Cornelius the centurion and his household, he first converted them to Christ, ib. 10.44 ff., and as in his outstanding apology he was the first to open the door of the Gospel so freely and solemnly to them, saying: <i>You know that in the early days God chose among you that the Gentiles should hear the word of the Gospel from my mouth and believe</i>, ib. 15.7, the promise nevertheless pertains not to Peter alone, but to the whole Church to be built above him, and indeed to all the Apostles who would be the architects of the building. But the words <i>You are Peter</i> amount to the same as if Christ had said: You are <i>built upon the rock [πετρωμένῳ]</i>. You have communion with the Rock that gives life and keeps unto eternal life. Although this was said to Peter alone, it is nonetheless a word in the manner of which Christ addresses not only Peter but all the faithful, giving them testimony to their spirit that they are sons of God.
