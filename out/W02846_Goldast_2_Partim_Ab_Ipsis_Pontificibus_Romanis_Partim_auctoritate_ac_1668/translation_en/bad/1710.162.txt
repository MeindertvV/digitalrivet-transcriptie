[12] 33. Here the question is raised whether one may appeal from any heretical sentence of the Pope. It is answered under a twofold opinion, and he pursues the second, namely that one may appeal from every sentence of the Pope, whatever heresy it contains, whether explicitly or implicitly condemned. This is proved by four reasons.
[13] 34. He inquires whether, if the Pope did not pronounce some heresy definitively but merely preached or taught it publicly, one may appeal from him. Here it is noted concerning heresy, the opposite of which one is bound to believe explicitly or implicitly, and this is answered
[14] 35. He gives one objection, proving that one must not appeal from him on account of such a teaching, to which an answer is also given in the same place, where it is also noted what kind of appeal such an appeal may be or become.
[15] 36. Here the following question is put: whether Catholics are to defend those who attack a heretical Pope, and it is answered under a twofold opinion. The first is proved in this chapter by two reasons; the second is proved in chapter 37 of this work by nine reasons.
[16] 37. Here the second opinion is stated and proved by nine reasons, showing that those who attack a heretical Pope, under due circumstances, are to be defended by Catholics even if they do not appeal.
[17] 38. He sets out four objections against the ninth reason of the immediately preceding chapter, namely the assertion that works of mercy are to be bestowed on all, which are answered in the immediately following chapter.
[18] 39. He gives replies to the objections of the preceding chapter, where three points of great note are also laid down: how works of mercy are to be bestowed more readily, and who and when men are bound to works of mercy.
[19] 40. Here another reason is assigned, which is the tenth, for the second opinion recited in chapter 37, proving that every Catholic who attacks in such a way is bound, by necessity of salvation, to defend in whatever way is possible for him. This reason is drawn from what has been said in chapter 39, where objections were answered by three points of note, and from other matters stated there.
[20] 41. Here three other reasons are assigned, proving the same as before. Thus in number there are fourteen reasons proving that Catholics who attack a heretical Pope, even if they do not appeal, are bound to defend such persons.
[21] 42. He sets out one escape, namely that such a defence pertains only to those who have jurisdiction over the wrongdoers. This is proved by authorities and reasons, where it is also proved that a companion ought not to defend a companion, and consequently is not bound to; the opposite of this is proved in the immediately following chapter. Replies to these authorities and reasons are given below in chapter 44 of this work, at page 541.
[22] 43. Here the opposite is proved, namely that a companion ought to defend a companion, by very many authorities, six reasons, and five examples, at page 541.
[23] 44. He gives replies to the authorities and reasons of chapter 42, which prove the opposite of this, namely that a companion is not bound to defend a companion, at page 544.
[24] 45. Here is treated the third conclusion of chapter 17, namely that an appeal from a Catholic Pope on account of heresy must be admitted before it is established that the appeal is not legitimate; this is proved by six reasons. The opposite of this is proved in the immediately following chapter, namely that such an appeal must not be admitted. At the beginning of this chapter, however, it is set out how certain persons who do not know that the Pope is a heretic are nevertheless bound to defend those who attack him; this is answered below in chapter 50 of this work, at page 546.
[25] 46. Here the contrary conclusion is stated and proved by two reasons, namely that such an appeal against the supreme Pontiff must not be admitted. These are answered in the immediately following chapter, at page 549.
[26] 47. Here is given the reply to the reasons of the preceding chapter, which prove that such an appeal is not to be admitted; here note concerning ignorance, at page 550.
[27] 48. Here it is inquired by whom anyone may be suspended, and what is meant by the name of suspension, at page 551.
[28] 49. He says that the Pope is bound to admit such an appeal made against him, and this is proved by three reasons, at page 552.
[29] 50. He asks whether those who do not know the Pope to be a heretic are bound to defend those who attack him for heresy. This was asked above in chapter 55, and, taking the terms in a good modified sense, he answers under a conclusion that they are. This is proved by a single reason; in the following chapter ten others are set out, where also another conclusion is proved by others, namely that such persons, even if they are led to death, are to be freed by Catholics. Here it is also noted whether a robber led to death ought to be freed; note also in the same place what is to be presumed more readily in such an attack, at page 552.
[30] 51. Here are set out ten further reasons proving the conclusion laid down in the immediately preceding chapter, namely 50. Thus in all there are eleven reasons proving that those who do not know are bound to defend those who attack the Pope for heretical depravity, until it is established to them that the attackers act from a bad zeal, at page 555.
[31] 52. He raises the question concerning prelates who have subjects attacking the Pope for heretical depravity, whether they are bound to defend them. It is answered that they are, which is also proved by four reasons, at page 556.
[32] 53. Here it is proved that kings and the other powers are bound to the same, and this by a single reason, at page 557.
[33] 54. Here it is inquired whether kings and princes ought to take up arms for such a defence? It is answered that they should, and this is proved by three reasons, at page 558.
[34] 55. Here are answered the authorities and reasons, by which it is proved that appeal from the Pope is not lawful, set out in chapter 16, at page 558.
[35] 56. It is asked: if a Pope defamed by heretical depravity should block an inquiry to be made into him, what is to be done by Catholics? Here note concerning the laying of violent hands on the Pope or a cleric, how and when this may be done.
[36] 57. He teaches to whom it belongs to inquire into the truth about him, namely the supreme Pontiff, if he has been stained or gravely defamed by heretical depravity.
[37] 58. Here it is shown what kind and degree of infamy ought to be, on account of which an inquiry is to be made concerning the Pope. Here note concerning the origin of anyone's reputation, according to the distinction of persons, and how a judge ought to conduct the process in the first kind of reputation.
[38] 59. He shows that in that case the Pope is bound, by necessity of salvation, to submit himself to the judgment of his inferiors, if the accuser appears to be fit. This is proved by a single reason.
[39] 60. Here it is declared how the supreme Pontiff ought to conduct himself if he has voluntarily submitted himself to the judgment of arbiters and the accuser fails in the proof against him. Two conclusions are laid down: the first, namely that he is bound, by necessity of salvation, to clear himself if a grave scandal would be caused among Catholics, is proved by eight reasons.
