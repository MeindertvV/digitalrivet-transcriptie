[1] By Pollux, I have much to say to you, if I were given the chance. But I do not know whether you will hear these things, and if you do hear them it will be in vain: you have done something which, even if you were weary of it, you could not undo. A restorer ought to be like the founder, just as a destroyer ought to be like one who tears down. You, Christ, who are able, by whose permission all rule, both on earth and in the heights and depths, is held, you who hear this my complaint, and especially this public one, even when it is spoken in silence, hear us, we beg you, if it is just. I know indeed that it is written, <i>if I regard iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me</i>. With you, Christ, the truth of things is certain; with us, opinion is uncertain. By that standard, what we ask for is certainly fair, since among the many grievous burdens there is one that is unbearable: we are oppressed not only by a harsh and foolish load, but by a hateful and hostile one, so that the words of the Psalm have been fulfilled in us to the letter: <i>Thou hast turned us back from the enemy, and they which hate us spoiled for themselves; thou hast given us like sheep appointed for meat</i>. What follows is well known. For us this alone is enough cause for complaint and groaning. For if we look at the matter, we who were once lords of the nations, what else have we now become but sheep for slaughter? Would that we were sheep yielding wool or milk; we are sheep for meat. We are gnawed at though we are willing, we are consumed, we are stripped of our skin by those who, if we should begin to resist, would look upon us as sheep before lions, and this would not seem strange to them, since they have used us for so long. Now our sluggishness supplies this boldness of cowardice: they devour our patience, and they hate it with an utterly impious rage. And, what you may find most astonishing, those whom they despise from outside they fear within. Contempt is feigned; fear is real. There are a thousand proofs of hatred and a thousand of dread. I will add one thing that combines both, which, though it was hidden before, has broken out so that it is known not only at Babylon but further away. At the time when that Pontifex Maximus, who had chosen for himself, in his old age, the task of overturning Italy, and above all this city of the Milanese, as a priestly campaign, raged with complete hatred against both, and, as Christian father of Christians, behaved as though this were not Italy but Syria or Egypt, not Milan but Damascus or Memphis. And when he had sent into these lands, as a holy and pious work, one son from the sacred college of the fathers, (as many said, his son; and the great resemblance in reputation and the fierceness of his character supported this), not an Apostolic man but one equipped in the manner of a plunderer, with the signs not of virtues and miracles but of the camp, and with extraordinary legions, as though he had destined him not as a second Peter but as a second Hannibal. In this war Almighty God, in his own way, resisted pride, favoured the humble, and openly fought for righteousness. There was one man from that same flock, and he too was insatiable in hatred against us and infinitely proud, whose face I, a boy, then knew, and whom I cursed in my heart as much as my weak age allowed. This man, dear to all the Papists, once entered the secretary's room and found the Pontifex on the threshold, dismayed and anxious over the rumours. (For the onslaught of war, beyond hope, clung to this city, which at that time was indeed bare of walls; but the best kind of wall is a soldiery distinguished and armed with most brave leaders. So often already had they routed their besiegers; the besieged were already flooding the prisons with crowds of captives; the fields were already growing fat with the flesh of the slain.) In this state of affairs, seeing him more sorrowful than usual, and trusting in his close familiarity, he approached him thus: "I am amazed, most holy father; what is it? You who are most keen in all other matters, do you see so little in that one thing which is supreme and chief for us?" To this the Pontifex raised his head, pressed down by heavy cares: "Wake up," he said, "what is the matter?" Then that outstanding adviser said: "I know that you desire nothing so much as the devastation of Italy; for this we have spent men, wealth and counsels; for this we have almost wholly poured out the treasures of the Church, having undertaken an affair without a way out, unless another way is tried. Look now: that great array of wars, that point of our strength, is blunted at the very gates of Milan, which your flatterers declared to be like some one of our cities, but which in fact has proved to be superior to all. When we were conquering Italy, we are conquered by a single Italian city. But if you will, there is another, far easier way." "What way?" said the Pontifex. "Tell me quickly. For this I wait, this I desire, this is the one thing for which I would bargain away body and soul." The other replied: "You can do all things, and whatever you command is settled. Why then do you not snatch away both the Papacy and the Empire from the city of Rome and from Italy? And transfer that Cahors, our homeland, that is, to Gascony? It is no great labour. Say the word and it will be done, and there is no need for arms, in which we are far inferior. You will triumph over your enemies with a word. Thus you will also adorn us with new insignia, the summit of affairs being transferred to us, and by a double light you will bereave the hated race." Roused by this, the Pontifex, his anger turning to laughter, said: "You have deceived me until now; I had not yet recognised that you are mad, or do you ignorantly fail to know that by this way, which you have subtly thought out, both I and those who succeed me would become bishops of Cahors, and whoever is the emperor would be prefect of Gascony, while he who presides at Rome would be, if spiritually, the Pope; if temporally, the Emperor? So while you think you are overthrowing the Italian name, you raise it up and restore it to its ancient dignity. Therefore let us, while it is granted to us from above, hold the reins of the Roman Pontificate, and let us be intent on this with all zeal, lest perhaps some day an Italian hand should seize its right, which, how long it can be prevented, is uncertain. Let us not quarrel over titles; whether we will or not, Rome will be the head of affairs." When these things had been heard, that clever fool blushed. But I am even forced to approve the wicked mind of the Pontiff, a mind which, although it blazed with hatred against us undeservedly, nevertheless remembered and knew where the height was founded from whose summit it took pride, and saw that the foundations were close to ruin and shaking. So he judged that he must keep quiet, and rejoice in silence, as if at the successes of brave men.
