[1] ## BOOK VI. On the punishment and penalties of heretics, and especially of the Pope, should he become a heretic 507. This book has two parts: the first deals with the Pope falsely defamed of heresy, up to chapter 66; the second with the same man truly defamed.
[2] Chap. 1. Whether the Pope has a superior
[3] judge on earth. Four ways of answering are set out here.
[4] There are two principal ones, however, and the first way of speaking
[5] is proved by six authorities
[6] and three reasons, to which an answer is given below in chapter 26 of this book,
[7] namely that the Pope has no superior on earth. 501
[8] 2. Here the opposing opinion is set out, namely
[9] that the Pope has a judge on earth, even an ordinary one. There are two ways of positing this.
[10] The first way is proved by a single reason,
[11] whose major premise is proved in chapter 3,
[12] namely that the Pope has no coercive jurisdiction.
[13] The second reason for proving the same thing is noted
[14] in chapter 4. The third and fourth are set out in chapter 5,
[15] and are also proved there by the example of John 12, chapter 508.
[16] 3. Here the minor premise assumed in the reasoning for the first opinion, in the preceding chapter, is proved,
[17] namely that the Pope has no coercive jurisdiction over others. This is proved by reasons and authorities, and it is very much to be noted. 508
[18] 4. Here is set out the second principal reason for proving the first opinion recited in chapter 2,
[19] namely that the Pope has a superior on earth, and this especially because such a one is some secular person. 511
[20] 5. Here is set out the third principal reason for proving that some secular person, and especially the Emperor, is the ordinary judge of the Pope, and likewise the fourth. 511
[21] 6. Here the contrary is argued, namely
[22] that no secular person, not even the Emperor, is the ordinary judge of the Pope. This is proved by authorities and five reasons in this chapter and the following one. These are answered in general in chapters 8 and 9, and individually in chapter 9 in particular. 512
[23] 7. Here the same thing is proved as in the preceding chapter. 513
[24] 8. He answers in general, under a twofold opinion, the reasons and authorities adduced in chapters 6 and 7; in this chapter he begins to answer the first. 513
[25] 9. Here, specifically, each of the authorities and reasons adduced in chapters 6 and 7, which prove that no secular person, not even the Emperor, is the ordinary judge of the Pope, is answered. 514
[26] 10. Here it is proved by reasons, authorities and examples that the Pope, falsely accused or defamed of the crime of heresy, is not subject to the judgment of the universal Church. These too are answered in chapter 63 of this book. 515
[27] 11. Here the opposite is argued, namely that the Pope, defamed of heresy, is subject to the judgment of the universal Church, and this by five reasons. 517
[28] 12. Here it is treated whether a general council has power over a Pope defamed of heresy, and it is proved by five reasons that it does not. These are answered below in chapter 64 of this book. 517
[29] 13. Here it is proved by two reasons and some authorities that the Pope defamed of heresy is subject to a general council. 518
[30] 14. He sets out one way of speaking about a Pope so defamed: that in such a case not only a general council but also the bishops have power to inquire into him. He assigns the cause and the manner of this. 518
[31] 15. Here it is shown that Catholics have the power to inquire into the truth concerning a Pope defamed of the crime of heresy, and that an inquiry is to be made about him. 519
[32] 16. Here, in sequence, it is treated of appeal from the supreme Pontiff, for which there are two ways of speaking. The first is that in no case is appeal against the supreme Pontiff to be admitted. This is proved by two authorities and legal reasons, to which an answer is given in chapter 55. The second way of speaking is set out in the following chapter, namely 17. 521
[33] 17. He sets out the opposite opinion, placed in the preceding chapter, under three conclusions. The first of these has three parts, which are treated in this chapter in their order; the second is treated in chapter 8 and following; the third in chapter 45. 522
[34] 18. Here it is set out that one may appeal from a heretical Pope. Note the manifold meaning and usage of the word appeal, and what an appeal is. 522
[35] 19. Here it is proved that one may in some case appeal from a heretical Pope, by 12 reasons. Note that many difficulties arise from this term, which are treated below in chapters 23 and following, up to chapter 28. 524
[36] 20. He adduces an objection against the conclusion and the case set out in the preceding chapter. To this a twofold answer is given. The first is set out in this chapter; the second is found below in chapter 28, where, in answering, note also that sometimes in a case of faith one may appeal to an unbeliever. 526
[37] 21. He raises an objection from what was said in chapter 20, where it can be inferred that one may appeal to a heretical Pope, and consequently from him, which seems contrary to what was said in chapter 18 of this book. 526
[38] 22. Here are set out four objections, or counter-arguments, against what was said in the answer of the preceding chapter to the objection raised there. 527
[39] 23. Here it is asked why it is said, in the position of those who hold that one may, or it is expedient to, appeal from the Pope in a case of heresy interposed, that three reasons are assigned for this. 
[40] 24. Here it is investigated why it is said in the conclusion, that it is lawful or expedient, which is laid down in chapter 18, where it is proved that it is not necessary to appeal from such an unjust sentence. This is proved by five reasons, to which an answer is given below in chapter 29. 529
[41] 25. He inquires into what was said above about the manner of speaking on appeal, namely why the conclusion is modified to say that it is not necessary to appeal according to the form of law. Here too a distinction is drawn concerning an appeal made against the faith. The answer to the form of the question is given below in chapter 27 of this book. 530
[42] 26. He assigns two reasons why no special statute in law has been stated about the manner of appealing from a heretical sentence of the Pope; two causes are assigned. 531
[43] 27. Here is set out the first response to the form of the question posed in chapter 25. 531
[44] 28. Here a second answer is given to the objection raised in chapter 20 against the arguments made in chapter 19. Here it is noted that an appeal from the sentence of a heretical Pope in the common form is necessary, even within the time fixed by law. This is proved by three reasons, to which an answer is given in chapter 30 of this book. 532
[45] 29. Here are set out the answers to the reasons of chapter 24, which prove that it is not necessary to appeal from the unjust sentence of a heretical Pope.
