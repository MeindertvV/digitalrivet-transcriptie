[1] ## BOOK FIVE of the first part, On those who can be stained by heretical depravity.
[2] Chap. I. Whether a Pope canonically elected can be made a heretic? That he can is shown first by authorities, then by examples. This is also shown in the second chapter. 467
[3] 2. In which it is proved that a Pope canonically elected can be made a heretic, by examples. 468
[4] 3. In which the same conclusion is drawn, namely that a Pope canonically elected can, while remaining Pope, err from the faith and become a heretic. This is proved by 15 arguments. 470
[5] 4. In which the opposing opinion is set out, arguing that a Pope canonically elected cannot, while remaining Pope, err from the faith and become a heretic. This is proved by 8 arguments, to which the following chapter replies. 473
[6] 5. In which replies are given to the arguments of the preceding fourth chapter. 479
[7] 6. Where the second question of this book is put, namely whether the college of Cardinals can be stained by heretical depravity. It cannot. This is proved here by ten arguments, to which chapter 8 of this book replies. 476
[8] 7. In which, according to the opinion of others, ten arguments are advanced to the contrary, proving that the college of Cardinals can be tainted by heretical depravity. These are answered in chapter 9 of this book. 477
[9] 8. Solves the arguments of the sixth chapter, holding that the college of Cardinals can err. Note here that the Roman Church and the apostolic see are in many ways su[?]. 
[10] 9. Solves the arguments of the seventh chapter, defending the first opinion, that the college of Cardinals cannot be made heretical. 480
[11] 10. In which another question arising from the preceding discussion is put, namely whether the college of Cardinals can be stained by heretical depravity together with others, and on this two opinions are set out. 480
[12] 11. In which the third principal question of this book is put, namely whether the Roman Church or the apostolic see can be infected with heretical depravity. On this there are four principal assertions, but it is subdivided into two. 481
[13] 12. In which it is proved, by two arguments, that the universal Church can be called the Roman Church. 481
[14] 13. In which it is proved that the Roman Church, as distinguished from the whole congregation of the faithful, cannot err against the faith. This is proved by eight arguments and certain authorities, to which a reply is given below in chapter 14. 481
[15] 14. In which another question arising from the third principal question is put, namely by what means the Roman Church obtained primacy over all others. On this he lays down five assertions or conclusions, which support the assertion of the preceding chapter, and which are proved separately in the following chapters. 483
[16] 15. In which the first conclusion is put and proved, namely that the blessed Peter did not hold primacy over the other apostles by the ordinance of Christ, by three arguments and some authorities. The opposing opinion to this is set out immediately in the following chapter, 16. 483
[17] 16. In which the contrary opinion is set out, and then argued against, proving that the blessed Peter held primacy over the other apostles by the ordinance of Christ; and this by five arguments. 485
[18] 17. In which the second, third and fourth of the aforesaid assertions are argued for, and especially the fourth. 486
[19] 18. In which the fifth and sixth assertions are argued for. 487
[20] 19. Where the contrary assertion or opinion on the primacy of the Roman Church is set out, namely that the Roman Church did not receive primacy over the other churches from the Emperor Constantine, but had it from the general councils. 487
[21] 20. Where it is deduced from authorities that the Roman Church had primacy over the other churches immediately from Christ, through the Gospel, and before the ascension. 487
[22] 21. In which it is proved that the Roman Church had primacy over all other churches immediately from Peter, who transferred it to the Roman Church. Once this is proved, three corollary conclusions are drawn. Afterwards it is noted whether the see could now be transferred from Rome to another city; and it is answered according to the opinion of some, to which the opposite is said to be yes, the second opinion. 488
[23] 22. In which he returns to the decision of the preceding principal third question, setting out and proving the contrary of the earlier assertion, namely that the Roman Church, which is distinct from the whole congregation of the faithful, can err against the faith; and this he indeed proves here. 
[24] 23. In which he proves the assertion of the preceding chapter by nine other difficult arguments, namely that the Roman Church, taken specially or in part, can err against the faith. 490
[25] 24. In which the thirteen arguments of chapter 13 are answered, which prove that the Roman Church, taken in part, cannot err against the faith. 492
[26] 25. In which the fourth principal question is put, namely whether a general council of the Church can slip into heretical depravity. That it can is argued here by five arguments, and in the following chapter by four examples. 494
[27] 26. In which the same conclusion is proved, namely that a general council can err against the faith; this is shown by four examples. 495
[28] 27. Where the opposing assertion, namely that a general council cannot err against the faith, is argued against by seven arguments, to which the following chapter replies. 496
[29] 28. In which, against the arguments of the preceding chapter proving that a general council cannot err against the faith, those holding the contrary reply. 497
[30] 29. Where the treatment of the fifth assertion mentioned above in the first chapter of this book is taken up, on which a fifth principal question may be formed, namely whether the whole multitude of the faithful can be made heretical. In order to decide this, he first treats this question: <i>whether the whole clergy can be disgraced by heretical falsehood</i>; for which this conclusion is put: <i>that the whole multitude of clerics can err against the Catholic faith</i>, and consequently <i>that the whole clergy is not that church which cannot err against the faith</i>, which he proves by 10 arguments. 498
[31] 30. He sets out the opposing opinion, namely that the whole clergy cannot err against the faith. Here also this conclusion is put: <i>that clerics alone are the church</i>, which is proved by seven arguments, to which the following chapter replies. 500
[32] 31. In which the arguments of the preceding chapter are answered, those proving that <i>the whole clergy is the church</i>, which cannot err against the faith. 501
[33] 32. In which it is proved by three arguments that the whole multitude of men, both clerics and laymen, can err against the faith, and that the Catholic faith is preserved among women. 503
[34] 33. In which the opposite is argued by two arguments: that the whole multitude of men, both clerics and laymen, cannot err against the faith. 304
[35] 34. Where the two preceding arguments are answered, by which it is proved that not the whole multitude of men can err against the faith. 504
[36] 35. Where it is proved by five arguments that the whole multitude of the faithful, who have the use of reason, can err against the faith. 505
