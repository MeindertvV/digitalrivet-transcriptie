[1] Against the wretched avarice of the devil, who snatches the goods of the Church to himself and, with the apostate Julian, miserably destroys and ruins our schools and churches. Sermon 1: they withhold and grudge the church goods and hoard them away; and on the other hand they let the pastors have them for their various luxury and idleness, or take them for themselves, and put in unworthy persons, or sell them as hereditary property, pawn them, or give them away. This is so common, even among those who want to be the most excellent Evangelicals, that people no longer count it a sin, etc. Sermon 11: we will not yet acknowledge it and do penance. O the way of wickedness! Everyone now is a good Evangelical, because he may take the goods of churches and monasteries to himself. Once he has them, his Gospel is finished. No one asks any more after the parson or the sermon; it is all one to him whether a man preaches or does not preach. They let parishes, schools and churches be laid waste. If anyone formerly broke open some church out of need and poverty, or took from it a chalice or other silver vessel, he had to be a sacrilegious man, that is, a church thief. They quartered him and broke him on the wheel, so that all his bones and limbs were broken, and then they laid him on the wheel for the birds and the air to devour. But the great church thieves of today, who walk about in gold and silver and eat and drink out of the church as the Chaldean king Belshazzar did, no one may say a word against them for behaving so badly. They send their poor and needy off with seals and letters to other people, who are frightened enough of them and have enough poor folk to take from, etc. These are truly the great thieves, who condemn the small ones to the gallows. Even if they go unpunished here, they will nonetheless not escape God's judgement, as Belshazzar did not. I do not, however, mean this of the Christian magistracy and the pious; for (thanks be to God) some of them are to be found about whom one need not sing and say what is said of the Pope and the other great church thieves. "He has scattered abroad and given to the pimps," and "to the whores," of the Christians' goods. Rather, truly: "He has scattered abroad and given to the poor." For the goods of the Christian churches, which at the foundations and monasteries have been abused and used for all kinds of idolatry, they call by the name of "better use" and "proper service": they wish to turn them to a better use and to the right service, that is, to establish Christian preachers' foundations, schools and hospitals, to make stipends for poor scholars, students and preachers, to give dowries and portions from them to poor serving maids, and everywhere to promote and maintain parishes and schools, the foundations and houses, and whatever else belongs to Christian order and government, so that no one among us goes hungry and has reason to complain. A magistracy that does this is highly to be praised, for it acts in a Christian and right manner. It takes away the abuse and restores the right use. For this was the purpose for which the foundations and monasteries were endowed in former times by pious lords and princes, that they should be schools of Christian teaching and of right learning, and that the goods given to them should belong to the poor. Therefore whoever embezzles them is surely a thief and a murderer. Juellus, bishop of Salisbury in England, in a sermon before Queen Elizabeth, on Psalm 19:9, "The zeal of thy house hath eaten me up," p. 138: "the cause why the church of God is so forsaken is the want of zeal in those who should, either out of their courtesy or according to their ability, be the fosterers of learning, and increase the livings where occasion is, and give hope and comfort to learned men. What did I say, increase? Nay, the livings and provisions which were formerly given to this use are taken away. Have patience, if any such be here, as I well know there are, whom these things touch. Suffer me to speak the truth: it is God's cause. The livings of those who are in the ministry are not in the hands of those to whom they are due, etc. And this is done not in one place or in one country, but throughout England. A gentleman cannot keep his house unless he has a parsonage or two in farm for his provision. O merciful God, where will this grow in the end? If the misery which this plague works reached only to one age, it would be tolerable. But it will be a plague to posterity; it will be the decay and desolation of God's Church." Robert Bolton, the most grave of the English preachers, in a sermon on 1 Corinthians 1:26, which he preached before the royal judges, names some other writers on this subject, and, what is more, others who first held the goods of the Church and then candidly restored them. "Nay, too many of those who keep back the Church's patrimony will neither restore it to its proper native use nor (which is very lamentable) part with a little portion of a large revenue of that kind towards a competency. Before you receive encouragement to go on in this course with comfort, I pray you procure for us (from your partakers, if there be any such) answers to those many learned treatises extant upon this argument, and (for anything I know) utterly unanswered: especially those of Mr. Bernard, Dr. Selater and Dr. Field. I know well that some excellent spirits of late, merely out of the gracious freeness of their truly noble dispositions, to their great honour and the adorning of their profession, have given back to the Church for ever (I mean nothing about buying in impropriations, one of the most glorious works of that kind, for anything I know, that was ever undertaken in this kingdom) diverse church livings, some an hundred pounds a year, some six or seven score, some threescore, some more, so many as amount to the value of above seven hundred pounds yearly. But I must tell you also: they are only such as you miscall Puritan gentlemen (for I neither hear nor know of any other that stirs this way), and how few such are to be found in a country, every eye spiritually enlightened may clearly see and heartily bewail. For I mean none but such as are (in true search and censure) God's best servants and the King's best subjects." The same man commends highly his patron, the judge Augustine Nicolls, in the funeral notes: "his singular integrity and honourable purpose in disposing of those ecclesiastical livings he had in his power." And in this point I myself can say more than anyone who has tasted deeply of his worthy dealing in this way. When I never sought after it, as is famously known, nor thought of any such thing, he sent for me and bestowed most freely that which I presently enjoy. Although every patron ought to do this in proportion, yet the horrible corruptions abroad in the world in such cases make a duty, as it were by a kind of antiperistasis, a transcendent virtue. And this was not all. Though encroachments upon the Church are like the breaches of the sea, a thousand to one never return, yet he restored to a farthing all that had for a long time been detained from the Church, and parted with it most freely, though he had as much wit and power as any other to have continued it, had he pleased. And I say ecclesiastical livings, though I instance only one, because I partly knew his purpose for the rest. For he gave me himself this message for as worthy and reverend a man as I know unpreferred in this land: that he would come to him, and he would give him the first that fell, and for no other reason in the world but because he heard he was a reverend and worthy man. Now lay these things to the practice of the times, wherein there is such sinful and simoniacal packing together, compacting, secret covenanting with the party or friends for present money or afterwards gratifications; some part of the tithes or of his own must be reserved to the patron, or he must be the farmer at his own price; or a wife pinned upon the sleeve of the parson, as they contemptuously speak (an abased and unworthy respect); or the like wretched combinations to help one another towards hell. My unacquaintance must excuse my ignorance of the terms. And then tell me whether this was not a noble part in him, worthy of the imitation of the best. I am persuaded in this point that he might be a pattern not only to all here present whom it might concern (though I look upon the faces of some who have also dealt very nobly this way), but to all the patrons in England. Be pleased then, you who loved him, to tread in his steps herein, and the rather because your unconscionableness in so high and important a point, for the glory of God and the good of the Church, may bring upon your own heads, your houses and posterity the curse of God in the meantime, but also a company of poor souls cast away by reason of your corruption, who will against you at that last and great day cry out upon you before the face of God, angels and men, that you were the men who for a little bloody gain put upon them an ignorant, idle, dissolute, non-resident, or in some way unfaithful minister (for it is too common that those who enter corruptly deal unconscionably in their places), whereby they must now perish everlastingly; whereas if you had been honest and uncorrupt, there had been hope they might have lived in the endless joys of heaven. And what a vexing cry will that be in the ears of all sacrilegious church-robbers, of a damned wretch in hell, when he shall complain everlastingly that his soul had been saved if such a man had not been simoniacal." Likewise Thomas Adams, also an Englishman, in a sermon on John 12:6, which was translated into Dutch under the title "The White Devil," inveighs against these abuses of ecclesiastical goods. The unhappy clergy beg in the streets. Others claim for their own uses all that belongs to the Levites." "This inconvenience could easily be remedied if your Majesty's laws, still in force, were executed." Controversies General, the fifth question, sixth point, where he disputes about the maintenance of ministers and about tithes: "It must also be provided that the gifts and legacies bestowed upon the Church be for the maintenance of piety and true religion and for good uses, not to nourish idolatry and superstition. And to this end he brings forward an argument ad hominem, since the Papists themselves sometimes consent that some of their goods be converted to better uses. Council of Cologne, part 9, chapter 9, canon 21: "Those so-called brotherhoods which are made in churches in honour of certain saints have indeed the appearance of piety, but as they are kept at this time, and in which both clergy and laity are chiefly given to revelries and drunkenness, and sometimes are also intent upon various conspiracies against the authorities, we would rather have them abolished, and their revenues spent on the uses of the poor." The same man, in his commentary on Exodus 20, on the eighth commandment, refers to sacrilege the case where church goods are employed for another end than that for which they were first given to the Church, which was to maintain the ministry of the Word of God and the sacraments, to relieve the poor, and to maintain the buildings of the Church. In the History of the Movements of the Kingdom of Scotland, p. 5, we read: "And because in those assemblies it was often seriously deliberated about vindicating the tithes and other ecclesiastical revenues, which certain private magnates had occupied beyond right and equity, and had turned without any appearance of right into the private patrimony of a few, therefore some of them, who understood that they possessed church goods by no just title, devised this art so that their clients and kinsmen, the pastors, might be raised to the titles of bishops, to whom a tiny portion of the revenues was granted, so that the rest might fall to them as plunder." And on p. 17, concerning the succession of Charles after the death of King James: "since the most pious king had especially given a remarkable example of the attitude he held towards the clergy, both in England and in Scotland, by that truly royal decree concerning the revocation of donations of church goods, above all tithes, which had been granted to certain nobles by the gift of a parent when a minor. And although the matter did not achieve the success such an excellent attempt deserved, nevertheless some advantage accrued to certain churchmen who showed themselves favourable to the machinations of the bishops." Certainly King Charles showed many times, and most often, how unwelcome to him was the usurpation of church goods by private persons and their application to uses other than ecclesiastical. In letters given to Parliament in the year 1647, in November, and in the Royal Image: "I thank God that, as no man lies more open to the sacrilegious temptation of usurping the Church's lands and revenues (which, issuing chiefly from the Crown, are held of it, and can legally revert only to the Crown with my consent), so I have always had such a perfect abhorrence of it in my soul that I never found the least inclination to such sacrilegious reformings. Yet no man has a greater desire to have bishops and all churchmen so reformed that they may best deserve and use not only what the pious munificence of my predecessors has given to God and the Church, but all other additions of Christian bounty. But no necessity shall ever, I hope, drive me or mine to invade or sell the priests' lands, which both Pharaoh's divinity and Joseph's true piety abhorred to do. So I think it unjust, both in the eye of reason and of religion, to deprive the most sacred employment of all due encouragements, and, like that other hard-hearted Pharaoh, to withdraw the straw and increase the task, so pursuing the oppressed Church as some have done, to the Red Sea of civil war, where nothing but a miracle can save either it or him, who esteems it his greatest title to be called, and his chiefest glory to be 'The Defender of the Church, both in its true faith and its just fruitions, equally abhorring sacrilege and apostasy.' I had rather live, as my predecessor Henry III sometimes did, on the Church's alms, than violently take the bread out of bishops' and ministers' mouths." Again: "which (however others may esteem it) to me is no less sin than sacrilege, or a robbery of God, the giver of all we have, of that portion which devout minds have thankfully given back to him, in giving it to his Church and prophets, through whose hands he graciously accepts even a cup of cold water as a libation offered to himself." Already in the year 1674. The author of the book "Ecclesiastical Discipline, and a full and clear explanation of the English Church's departure from that error, from the Word of God," printed at La Rochelle, p. 95, said: "For our age has many such soldiers, many Dionysiuses, who think that a golden cloak, which is convenient neither for summer nor for winter, will be most useful and most convenient for them at all times. But if they will attend more carefully to what we say, they will understand that this discourse in no way favours their desires, their avarice and their rapacity. For it is not here a question of despoiling bishops, nor of plundering church goods and dividing them to the most unworthy; rather, on the contrary, it is that the poverty of the many may be relieved by the affluence of the few, by maintaining hospitals and almshouses, and (what is the main thing) by establishing the ministry throughout the whole kingdom. So from this discourse there is not only nothing for them to hope or expect, but rather something to fear, if perhaps they should at any time usurp the patrimony of the Church. For they must either restore these things, so that the churches may be provided with fit teachers, or they will bind themselves to God for as many souls as shall perish in their ignorance through those destitute teachers." The Orders of our provinces, in the district of Holzminden [Silva-Ducis], on 11 June, in the year 1630, decreed as follows: "Since it has come to our knowledge that several goods, purely ecclesiastical, are being obscured under the pretext that they belong to lay right, we have decided that all persons of whatever condition who possess any vicarages or benefices shall, in the altar-places that were founded at Holzminden, show us, or the questor of church goods at Holzminden, their original foundations or an authentic copy, together with a designation of the goods belonging to them, within the space of the following months, under penalty, etc." The same thing, more strictly, again in the year 1636, 6 August. See the Codex of the edicts of the Orders, part 2, pp. 220 and 418. Namely, the copies of the donations are to be inspected, so that it may appear from them what sort of goods are in question, whether they are ecclesiastical, or are owed to the ecclesiastical ministry, or to the poor, or to the schools. Erasmus, in the Adages: "He has the gold of Toulouse." This opinion still lives among the common people, who think that all who lay violent hands on sacred things perish miserably. Henningus Brosius, a theologian of Halberstadt, wrote a treatise, "The Gold of Toulouse," that is, a short treatise and instruction concerning the spiritual and secular goods of the Church, also concerning the happiness and punishment that follow the robbing of the Church. In chapter 3, answering the objection that those goods are idolatrous, given under the Papacy for superstitious ends, he says: "If now, however, through the error of those centuries, through the opinion of merit and the fear of purgatory, something incorrect has come about among our dear ancestors ... (a black-letter passage continues in the same vein, copied as far as it is legible) ... that they are bound to maintain from those aforementioned goods, as the ministries of church and school were formerly provided, and afterwards also provided and maintained, whatever religion that may be. Whoever therefore does this, and abolishes that abuse and superstition that has crept in, does right and well on both sides, for he does not violate the purpose or promise who changes it for the better (Decretals, book 2, title 24, chapter 3). Just as a guild does not sin if it spends a legacy of twenty or thirty gulden, which the donor or testator otherwise assigned for an anniversary and a feast, on pious causes, and gives from it a coat, shoes or bread to poor folk, so neither does he act against the intention of the ancestors who removes error and superstition. To this opinion the learned lawyers also give assent, as can be seen in Chothmann, the second volume of Responsa, responsum 100, number 2, etc. To this also goes the opinion of the Protestants at Gildastus, the second volume of the Imperial Constitutions, folio 215, number 79. Over and beside this, Menelaus should also know that the Christian ancestors for the most part thought little of purgatory when they gave their goods to the churches. For just as Jacob (Genesis 28:20) made a vow, that if God would keep him and give him bread and clothing on his journey, and bring him back in peace to his father's house, he would give him a tenth of all his goods, so many of the Christian ancestors, in dangerous journeys by water and land, in sicknesses, in times of pestilence, in barren marriages and in other cases, made vows that if God would protect them on their ways, and give them health, marital blessing and children, then they would in return bequeath and appoint this or that to his honour. When now such a condition was fulfilled and their request granted, such a vow had also to be fulfilled and paid (Numbers 30:1; Deuteronomy 23:21). Hence the emperors Charles and Louis called the true church goods 'the vows of the faithful' (causa 16, q. 1, c. 59). And since many of the Christian ancestors also found that the blessing of the LORD had made them rich, they in turn bequeathed something to God's honour as a real testimony of their thankful minds. What moved the honourable and blessed Christoph von Hoym, Adolph and Christoph von Hoberodt, Lord Arnold Meier and others, to bequeath and bestow a sum for some evangelical preaching posts here a few years ago? Certainly nothing other than their thankful minds, and that God might grant their heirs more luck and blessing. Now as these and many others have had weighty reasons for setting aside something of their goods for pious causes, so the Christian ancestors three, four and five hundred years ago also had their weighty reasons for assigning something to such purposes. Therefore the best counsel in general is to keep silent about Baal's goods and purgatory, and to leave to God what is God's and give to God what is God's; then the conscience remains without burden, and one is then assured that among the lawful goods no eagle's feathers or gunpowder are mixed in, and that one's fire is not set to the stables. Some people still have so much Christian reflection that they think one must leave to God and the Church and the poor whatever has been given to them by the Evangelicals within the last hundred years. If this is right, why should the old goods, which were given two hundred, three hundred, four hundred, five hundred or more years ago, be of worse condition, and why should it be free to take those goods away from the churches? Both the earliest and the latest spiritual goods have acquired this kind, nature and property, that they can never again be used for secular purposes (Leviticus 27:28; Numbers 30:1). From which passages this canon was made (book 3 of the Decretals, title 19, chapter 3): "What has once been consecrated to God ought not to be transferred to human uses." But whoever allows himself to lust after it is made anathema, accursed and cursed, and must restore everything threefold (distinction 69, chapter 1; causa 17, q. 4, chapter 4 'scriptoris' and chapter 5 'eaunes'). He continues, chapter 5, p. 46. Doctor Sigfrid Sack writes in the fourth part of his sermons on the Gospels, folio 207, as follows: "Nordbert hung a curse on the foundation of the monastery of Our Lady in Magdeburg: anathema, maranatha, until the day of the Lord. That is: that he who alienates the goods shall be accursed and shall answer for it on the last day." Therefore (adds Sack) he lays hands on no one's church goods. "They are eagle's feathers and gunpowder. And wherever they come into an inheritance or an estate, they eat everything up, so that one thing vanishes with another and one does not know where it flies or flees." In the same place: The blessed Dr. Henry Petreus, who in the year 1610 promoted me to the then well-established monastic school at Walkenried, appended to Henricus Meibomius's Chronicle of Riddagshausen a treatise on spiritual goods, in which he mentions, on p. 3, that he read a deed of donation in which a curse was pronounced upon the robbers of churches that their lungs and liver should be consumed in their bodies. In the same place he also writes that the praiseworthy Landgrave Philip of Hesse, at the then alteration of the monastery at Heina, wished for the five sacrilegious men that God's hand should punish them with poverty, sickness, disgrace and shame. The godly abbot of Ilfeld, Thomas Stange, with great zeal wished upon the church-robbers at their end all kinds of misfortune, God's wrath, eternal damnation and unhappy progress in all their undertakings, as the highly deserving Mr. Neander bears witness, in the Epistle to the Nuremberg catechism in his Greek book, p. 3 (Tum. cap. 6). After that the Germans say in proverbial fashion that church goods are eagle's feathers. For just as eagle's feathers, according to Pliny (book 10, chapter 3) and the received opinion, consume all other feathers among which they are mixed, so church goods also devour all other goods among which they are mixed, by right or wrong, says Luther (chapter 25, folio 296). Besides, Luther in the third volume of the Jena edition, folio 247, cited this proverb about church goods: "It is not good to joke with the saints; they take revenge." This seems to be the proverb of Solomon, Proverbs 20:25: "It is a snare to a man to devour holy things." Moreover, it is proverbially said of spiritual goods: "The saints fetch back the wax." Further, the Germans also say in a proverb: "Church goods have iron teeth; they devour one thing with another." Finally, church goods are also called gunpowder. When gunpowder comes into a house, it throws house and yard into a heap; so church goods also throw house, yard and a whole family into a heap when they are brought unlawfully among other goods. In short, the sense of such proverbs is that in the end all church-robbers come to harm, so that for them and their children even all rightly acquired goods vanish from their hands and are scattered. This the dear ancients held to be so true that they said that if a man had only one stone from the church in his house under a wrong title, he would feel misfortune enough inside it. Therefore they used to wish their worst enemies: first, a bad neighbour; second, a green stone path before the door; and third, a stone from the church.
