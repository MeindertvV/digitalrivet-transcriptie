[1] § 3. At the end of the same D.3 he lies hidden under a cloud when he says that certain voluntary muscles do not simply obey the command of the will. For I ask, who are those? And it matters to the reader to be warned by at least one example, since no one can guess what he had in mind. But let us wait, certainly, until it suits him to explain himself. At D.4 he makes the same mistake when he says that the muscles are the proper instruments of voluntary motion. By the word "proper" he indicates what he means, but I would prefer that all the parts necessary for producing motion be set out, perhaps in this way, which will be explained more fully below. The spirits, the authors of motion, have their origin in the heart, receive their perfection in the brain, and are carried through the nerves, as through tubes, to the muscles. Finally, the muscles are the chief part of this action, namely in the more perfect animals. For in the imperfect ones there is something analogous. From this he would have received light for D.8, that it can happen that a muscle, unharmed in its substance, either does not perform its own function, or performs it badly, if it sees, or if it has no spirits, or has ill-disposed ones.
