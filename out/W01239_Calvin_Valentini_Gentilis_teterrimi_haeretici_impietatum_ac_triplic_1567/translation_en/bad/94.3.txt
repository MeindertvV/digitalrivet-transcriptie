[1] As for my saying first of all that the errors of Arius and Eutyches, and others besides, are to be condemned, I am moved less by the abuse of Stancarus, who condemns even the innocent as Arians, than by the words and letters of many good men, and by a certain tablet recently published among you. For the author of that tablet, while defending against Sabellius the true distinction of persons in the sacred Trinity, at the same time not obscurely takes the side of the new Arians, who make us three Gods, not in word but in fact. And since I hear that Valentinus Gentilis and Georgius Blandrata live among you, of whom the former has published elsewhere clear signs of his perverse faith concerning the Trinity, and of the latter an impious little book is extant in public, I have no rash fear that they may perhaps have breathed their poison on the author of that tablet and on some others as well. For the authors of the new Arianism are wont to insinuate themselves by various and wonderful arts, and they do not pour out all their poison at once but little by little. To strike the minds of the simple, they boast grandly and dramatically that they seek the glory of God alone, even if enemies, tumults, or indeed the collapse of the world should follow, provided, they say, that the glory of God and the truth of the Son of God are safe. <i>They wish to assert this only from the sacred writings. They have cast off the many delusions of the Fathers and trampled underfoot the trifles of the schoolmen; yet meanwhile, in this article of eternal life, they snatch up much from those same sources, and they darken the word of God with new names introduced from outside Holy Scripture.</i> I certainly praise the intention of whoever seeks the glory of God from the heart, and I approve that they wish to illustrate it from the sacred writings. Yet I think it must be carefully considered whether all the things some scatter about the Trinity as if they were oracles are confirmed by the sacred writings and illustrate the glory of God. For the dispute is not, as some suppose, about words; it concerns the things themselves. If we agree about these, we shall not allow the names essence or person, or others of the same kind that the ancients used, to stand in the way of concord. We are inquiring what the sacred writings teach about the divine majesty, and we want it carefully weighed whether what these names signify is handed down in them. If we agree on this, we will easily agree on the words too; but where there is disagreement about the things themselves, every change of the received names seems to us suspect and dangerous. I marvel that some of them object to us that we use names not found in the Scriptures, when they constantly have on their lips the names "essentiator" and "essentiated" of God, which they certainly did not receive from the prophets or apostles. Yet we do not blame them for using names foreign to the sacred writings, but for putting forward a doctrine foreign to them as well. For they seek a distinction of Father, Son and Spirit in the essence, so that this essence belongs to the Father alone, and the Son and the Holy Spirit have another. To prove this, they heap together all the passages in which Scripture speaks distinctly of the Father and the Son, adding many that have nothing to do with the matter at hand. But where mention is made of the one God, or the name God is put without any attribute, they say that only the Father is to be understood, because he is the one true God and [αὐτόθεος] God of himself. But by this reasoning, if the Father is one God, and the Son another God, and the Holy Spirit another God, and each has an essence distinct or separated from the other, there will be three gods altogether. Although the author of the tablet denies this, it nevertheless follows from his doctrine, and others of this faction say it openly and teach that the Trinity is a union of three essences, or of three distinct spirits. We, however, following not the delusions of the Fathers or the trifles of the schoolmen but the teaching of the sacred writings, teach that the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are three distinct hypostases [ὑποστάσεις], that is, as some of the ancients put it, subsistences or persons, each of whom is called God. And because the sacred writings proclaim that there is one God, we believe that the Father and the Son <i>and</i> the Holy Spirit are this one God, whom the Scriptures set before us to be believed and adored: so that there is one eternal, best, most wise, omnipotent, immense and immortal essence, in which, however, as he himself reveals it, we acknowledge and believe three hypostases [ὑποστάσεις] in the same Deity, as I said, not three names only but three persons, or three subsistent things; yet not three essences, since the hypostasis [ὑπόστασις], which we here call substance and others subsistence, differs greatly from the ousia [οὐσία], which we translate as essence.
