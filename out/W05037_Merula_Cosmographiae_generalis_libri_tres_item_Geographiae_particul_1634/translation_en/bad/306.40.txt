[1] From this city Antoninus describes three itineraries. Two run to <i>Castulo (Castona la veia)</i>, and the third to <i>Malaca (Malaga)</i>. The first, which goes to Castulo over 199 miles, passes through the towns <i>Calpurniana (Carpio now)</i>, <i>Vrgao (Argione)</i> and <i>Ituirgi (Aldea el rio)</i>. The second, over 68 miles, passes through <i>Epora (Montero)</i> and Vciense. The third, which leads to Malaca and runs 291 miles, goes through <i>Tugia, Fraxinum, Hellara, Acci (Guadix), Aiba, Urci (perhaps the Virgi of others, Vera in common usage), Turania, Murgi (Murga), Severanum (Motrel), Cariclum, Menoba, Malaca</i>. One may not unreasonably wonder at this, says Juan Vasco in his Chronicle of Spain, where he discusses the bishoprics of Spain: although Livy in his account of the civil war mentions many of the towns of Baetica, he nonetheless makes no mention of Corduba. Five leagues south of Corduba is <i>Mons Maior</i>, commonly called Monte Mayor. It is thought to be the Oulia of Ptolemy [Οὐλία], a town of the Turduli in Hispania Baetica; it is <i>Ulia</i> in Antoninus, and <i>Ulla</i> in the writers of the Alexandrian and Spanish wars, where the form is faulty. Today Villanueva is called <i>Ulla</i>, or <i>Veia</i>. In the town of Monte Mayor there are certainly very elegant monuments of ancient stones, which sufficiently show that the ruins of Ulia lie there. Hieronymus Surita states that he possesses a coin on which is stamped a most unsightly image of a man, above the crescent of the moon, and before the face an ear of grain without inscription; on the reverse are sprigs of olive, with the name of the town, ULIA. Ten miles from Corduba is <i>Mons Aureus</i>, commonly called <i>Montoro</i>, believed to be the <i>Epora</i> of Antoninus. Ptolemy wrongly gives Ἐβυρα [Ebyra] for Ἐμερα [Emera], since that place belongs not to Baetica but to Lusitania; worse still is <i>Ripepora</i> in the common editions of Pliny, where it is surnamed <i>Foederatorum</i>. An inscription, which the noble V. Augerius Busbequius communicated to the most illustrious Justus Lipsius after copying it on the spot, mentions the municipality of Epora and the bridge built by its bishop. It reads thus:

[2] ## IN MEMORY. DEDICATED. SINCE THE PUBLIC GOOD OF THE MUNICIPALITY OF EPORA HAS ALWAYS BEEN DEAR TO IT, RIGHTLY THIS STONE BRIDGE WHICH YOU SEE, OVERHANGING THE RIVER BAETIS, AND RIVALING THE BRIDGE OF TRAJAN, HAS BEEN BUILT AT GREAT EXPENSE, AS AN ETERNAL FAVOUR AND A MONUMENT OF THINGS DONE. THEREFORE TRAVELLERS NOW GO EASILY, AND, THE GREAT DANGERS BEING REMOVED, TREADING UNDER FOOT THE RAPID WAVES OF THE GURGITES, THEY SHALL HAVE THANKS FOR PERPETUAL SAFETY.

[3] I return to Hispalis, and from there, by Palatium, the bridge of Alcantarilla, and the towns of Cabeças and Nebrissa, I make for <i>the Temple of Lucifer</i>. The Latins named it so, in my judgement, because Strabo in book III translates it as the temple of Phosphorus [Φωσφόρου ἱερὸν]; and he adds that it was also called Αυχιμδοβιαω [Auchimdobiaō], that is, Doubtful Light. Today it is <i>S. Lucar de Barrameda</i>. Not far from here, about four leagues towards the south-east wind, is a town which the Spanish call <i>Xerez de la Frontera</i>, as if it were the beginning or the end of Spain on this coast. Navagero in his Itinerary thinks this is <i>Asta</i>, which Livy in book XXVIII, the author of the Spanish war, Pliny in book III (who surnames it <i>Regia</i>), and others call by that name. Strabo and Ptolemy write it Ἀστα [Asta], and Strabo places it with Nebrissa at the estuaries of the Baetis. Antoninus writes <i>Hasta</i> with an aspirate, in both printed and manuscript books. The author of the Spanish commentary also mentions A. Baebius, C. Flavius and A. Trebellius, Roman knights of <i>Asta</i>, who deserted to Caesar with almost all their silver. Moralis writes that <i>Asta</i> still keeps its name, or that the place is <i>Masa de Asta</i>, near the river Guadalete, as it is commonly called. Abraham Ortelius is not far from thinking that it was submerged along with the island of Tartessus at the mouth of the Baetis. The land of this town produces the most excellent and swiftest horses of all Spain; the common people call them <i>Genettas</i>. The nobles train them with remarkable skill and take pleasure in their running in the hippodromes, while they stage equestrian battles, in which they practise against each other with reeds and canes in place of spears, in the manner in which the Moors are accustomed to hurl spears and cast them at enemies. In their native tongue they call these games <i>Juegos de Cannas</i>. A hundred stadia from the mouth of the Baetis lies the town of <i>Chipiona</i>, which I do not doubt is the Καπίων πύργος [Kapiōn pyrgos] of Strabo in book III, that is, the Tower of Capion, ... For, as the same author adds, since the mud carried down by the river makes the shallow water deep, and the rocks hidden under the water project from the place, it needed some conspicuous sign. At the mouth of the river Lethe, which today is the Guadalete, or in Arabic <i>Bedalac</i>, is a town called <i>Portus S. Mariae</i>, commonly <i>El puerto de S. Maria</i>; it is the Μενεδίων Λιμὴν [Menedion Limēn] of Ptolemy and Strabo. Crossing the Lethe one comes to <i>Medina Sidonia</i>, which Clusius thinks is the Ἀνυδὸρ [Anydor] of Ptolemy. Heading farther south towards the Strait, there presents itself <i>Conil</i>, no mean town of Spain, six leagues from the city of Gades, most illustrious for the Lucas of Sidonia, who is also the parent of the Duke of Medina Sidonia; it has especially rich resources in the catching of various fish, above all the tunny, about which more fully elsewhere. <i>Conil</i> is considered by Georg Austriacus (in Abraham Ortelius) to be the Καρπία [Karpia] of Ptolemy, and the Καθαία [Kathaia] of Stephanus; it is <i>Carteia</i> in Livy, who in book XXVIII describes its position in this way: <i>While these things were going on at the river Belis, Laelius meanwhile, having been carried through the Strait into the Ocean with his fleet, approached Carteia. That city lies on the shore of the Ocean, where the sea first spreads out from narrow jaws.</i> It is also read in the Greeks as Ταρτησσὸς [Tartessos], as Strabo, Pliny and Pomponius bear witness; Ταρτήσιον ὄλβιον ἄστυ [Tartesion olbion astu], the wealthy city of Tartessus, is in Homer. Appian of Alexandria perhaps wrongly calls it Καρπησος [Karpēsos]. Some hold that the river Ταρτησσὸν [Tartessos], which afterwards was called the <i>Baetis</i>, as I said in chapter VII, gave <i>Tartessus</i> its name. Hence the region Ταρτησσὶς [Tartessis] in Eratosthenes, Artemidorus and others. Of <i>Tartessus</i> are these lines of Anacreon: "Would that I might again be at the temples of Amalthea, and again at Peucon and at the goddess of Tartessus, the queen" [Ἐ’γὼ τ᾽ αὖ ἔτ᾽ Ἀμαλθίης βυλοίμην κιεροῖς, ἔτ᾽ ἐπὶ Πευκονωδὶν καὶ ἐκάτην Ταρτησσᾶ Βασιλεῦον].
