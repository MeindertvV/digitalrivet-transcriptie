[1] V. <i>Leo</i>, Λέων [Leōn], al-Asad (الأسد); Avienus calls it <i>Flammiferum</i> (flame-bearing), and Maximianus <i>Affiliferum</i>. Cicero has <i>Tremulam quatiens è corpore flammam</i> (shaking a quivering flame from its body). Others mark it with different epithets taken from the nature of the animal. It looks toward the west. It sets and rises by the head. It has 27 stars in its figure and 8 outside it. Of these, the bright star of the first magnitude in its heart is called by the Greeks Καρδία Λέοντος [Kardia Leontos], "the Lion's Heart," from its place, as Geminus and Proclus testify; Latins call it <i>Cor Leonis</i>. In the Asterisms of Hipparchus it is Βασιλίσκος [Basiliskos]; and <i>Regulus</i>, because, as Proclus notes, astrologers attribute a kingly disposition to those born under it, however lowly their fortune. The star at the very end of the tail, ἐπ' ἀκρας τῆς οὐρᾶς [ep' akras tēs ourās], as Hipparchus says, is likewise named from its place, οὐρὰ Λέοντος [oura Leontos], <i>Cauda Leonis</i>, "the Lion's Tail"; to the Arabs it is ذنب الأسد [dhanab al-asad], and it is a star of the first class. Among the unnamed stars around the Lion, Hipparchus, in the Asterisms, calls the cloudy cluster that lies beyond the tip of the Lion and the Virgin's ... ἀμάρφωτοι [amorphōtoi, unformed], the Πλόκαμος [Plokamos], "the Lock." It designates the hair of Berenice, which Pliny (wrongly assigning it to the southern constellations) calls by that name, Book 2, chapter 70. Πλόκαμος [Plokamos], and the cloudy cluster called the Lock, is also the name Ptolemy the astronomer gives it; commonly it is called <i>Trica</i>. The story is this. When Ptolemy Euergetes, brother and also husband of Berenice, undertook a rather dangerous campaign against the Assyrians, she vowed that if he returned home safe she would cut off her hair. He did return, and she, true to her vow, dedicated her cut hair to Venus. When it did not appear in the temple the next day, the king was upset, and Conon the mathematician, seeking favour by flattery, declared that it had been taken up among the stars. Callimachus wrote a poem on this matter, and Euergetes, because of his unusual devotion to the liberal arts, was like a god to him. But the poem has perished through the ravages of time. What it was like Catullus shows, who made a Latin version of it. A fragment of the Greek poem survives in Theon's commentary on Aratus. When Aratus says that some stars scattered near the Virgin's constellation lack names and are ἀνώνυμοι [anōnymoi, nameless], Theon adds: "Others call them the spindles. But Conon the mathematician, to gratify Ptolemy, showed that the locks of Berenice had been placed among them. For Callimachus says somewhere: 'Conon placed me in the sky by day, the lock of Berenice, which shines clearly to all'" [οἱ δ' ἡλακατῶν αὐτὰς λέγουσι. Κόνων δ' ὁ Μαθηματικὸς Ἱπλεμαίω χαρίζεσθαι. Βερενίκης πλόκαμοι εἰς αὐτῶν κατεσήμησαν. τοῦτο γὰρ Καλλίμαχος πω φησιν].
