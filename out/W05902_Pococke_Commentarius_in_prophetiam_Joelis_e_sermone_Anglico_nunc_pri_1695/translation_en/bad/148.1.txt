Rather, one may infer from this that no name of this kind, in our language or any other, in their judgment exists which exhausts the whole force and <i>energeia</i> [ἐνέργεια] of that word, and there is no reason for us to look for one. St John himself, inspired by the divine Spirit, when he wished to use this name in another language, did not hesitate to express it by a periphrasis in <i>Rev. 1,4.</i> ὁ ὢν καὶ ὁ ἦν καὶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος [who is, and who was, and who is to come], <i>who is, and who was, and who is to come</i> (or ὁ ἐσόμενος, as in <i>Rev. 16,5.</i> <i>who is to be</i>). Nor does the form of this word stand in the way of this. For it embraces the letters and signs characteristic of all three tenses, present, past and future, and it denotes a being who now is, who was, and who will be, one and the same for ever. That they do not express it often in this way, but change it for another name which denotes absolute rule and dominion, seems to have been done under the example and authority of other early translators, who followed the same method of translating before them. The Seventy are an instance: disregarding the form and sound of the letters, they render it by no other name than the Greek κύριος [Lord]. Yet there is no doubt that they understood it in a wholly singular and more excellent sense when they render it thus, than when the word is used in the common way of men who hold supreme power. This is clear enough from the fact that Origen is reported to have written in the margin, opposite, that the Seventy noted the Hebrew letters J.H.V.H., יהוה, to warn the reader who is meant in that place; and, as is evident, by the same work he indicated the name. {b} See the preface to the Arabic Pentateuch in the Bodleian Library, and Procopius in Curterius on Isaiah. These letters, written at first out of reverence and good intention, led some into error, as the Arabic translator of this opinion reports: some Greeks, who were ignorant of the Hebrew letters and thought them to be Greek, extracted from them by wrong reading ΠΙΠΙ [Pipi], which they seemed to represent, and so took it as the name of God, Pipi. Following them, certain Syrians expressed it by ܦܗܝܦܝ <i>Fbiphi</i>, which they thought denoted الرب [the Lord], or السيادي <i>Arabo-Jamajo</i> [the heavenly God], as Bar Bahlul rendered it in his Syriac lexicon. That the name is, as they say, altogether <i>ἄρρητον</i> [unutterable], is also pleasing to not a few, even for this reason, that they lack proper letters to express its genuine sound, and no single name exhausts the meaning it expresses. The author of the Latin Vulgate was no less cautious when, in a similar way, he did not set that name down in its proper letters, but translated it by the Latin word <i>Dominus</i>. Yet one or another passage must be excepted, where he thought it better to express it so that it would plainly appear to be the proper name of God and peculiar to him. Nevertheless, he did not hesitate to abstain from the proper letters and to put another Hebrew name in their place, namely <i>Adonai</i>, as at <i>Exod. 6,3.</i> where the Hebrew has <i>my name</i> יהוה <i>Jehovah</i>, but he rendered it <i>my name Adonai</i>, which he also did in the apocryphal book <i>Judith 16,16.</i> This is no slight sign of the reverence by which he seems wholly to have abstained from pronouncing this name according to its proper letters, perhaps following the example of the Jews, who dare not utter it but still read in its place אדני <i>Adonai</i>, which also means Lord, except in those places where this name is joined to it, since in its place they then substitute אלהים Elohim, <i>God</i>. In this matter not only our countrymen but also various other scholars agree with them. Why the Jews abstain from pronouncing these letters, they give this reason: that the genuine pronunciation, which was indeed known and in common use for some time while the first temple stood, and also for a while in the second, afterwards utterly perished. Hence they dare not conform to the common manner of speech in this, greatly fearing that they might use it profanely or deprive God of his genuine name. Yet this incurs the mark of superstitious and needless fear among some learned men, who maintain that it can still be uttered with its genuine sound, and for that reason they do not refuse to read and pronounce it according to the letters and the vowels attached to them. Our countrymen, as we have noted, judged this lawful too; but the reason why they used it less often was either reverence, or the authority and example of the ancients. Hence the word <i>Dominus</i> pleased them more. Therefore, while others here render it <i>Adonai, Jehovah</i>, our countrymen have <i>Adonai, Lord, I will cry</i>.
