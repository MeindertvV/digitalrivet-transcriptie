[1] r) The Douai (Duacenses) version too. p) Kimchi too. q) The cultivators are so frustrated and deceived that they seem to be ashamed of their own lie. Ruff. See Kimchi and Drusius. r) Lively. s) Pet. a Figueiredo takes the word in this sense here, namely as the cause of the shame with which those were suffused who fell from their hope. t) Lively, Bochart. The verb <i>to be ashamed</i> is used only of <i>shame</i>, never of <i>to wither</i>, as when Lebanon is <i>ashamed</i>. Yet, although there is some difference between these meanings in sound, in sense it is slight or none. The wine was <i>put to shame</i>, dried up of its juice to such an extent that it could not at once fill the cultivator's expectation, since he hoped for a greater quantity than he received. Therefore, in whatever sense the reader takes it, proper or figurative, since the word admits both, there will be no difference in the matter. Castalio has <i>the wine was defiled</i>, so that the cultivators would thereby incur shame. The Hebrew אמלל יצהר, <i>Umlal ytshar</i>, <i>the oil <i>languishes</i></i>... The word means, as Abu Walid says, <i>weakness and languor</i>; or, as David Kimchi says, <i>weakness and cutting off</i>. The Greek renders it ὠλιγώθη, <i>it was diminished in quantity</i>, and the printed Arabic and the Chaldean paraphrast <i>the olives fell off</i>. So too Buxtorf. Others <i>the olives dropped off</i>, or <i>the olive trees</i>, and Mercerus <i>the olives were stripped of their leaves</i>; or, as in his notes on this passage, <i>the olive trees dropped off</i> (their leaves, of course); the Vulgate, <i>the oil has languished</i>. The Syriac, <i>the olive trees were cut off or destroyed</i>. The Arabic manuscript has אנקף אלרהן, which I suppose should be read <i>Enkaffal dohn</i> and translated <i>the oil has dried up</i>. Various recent translators have, for example, <i>the oil is broken</i>, <i>cut off</i>, <i>exterminated</i>, <i>weakened</i>, <i>deformed</i>, <i>perished</i>, <i>languished</i>, and <i>has languished</i>. In brief, the corruption of the oil seems to be indicated, neither whole nor so great in quantity that it could be customarily used either in the sacrifices offered to God or in their own manner of living. These three things, grain, wine and oil, were of such importance to them that without them they could neither perform those sacrifices which were ordained by the law and necessarily made from these very things, nor lead a pleasant life, since, as Kimchi says, they are רוב חיי האדם <i>the greater part of human life</i>: bread, namely, to strengthen the heart, wine to gladden, and oil to give a pleasing appearance. And since they were so miserably deprived of these, there could not but arise the greatest occasion for mourning, not only for the priests and ministers of God, but also for men of all ranks among them. Therefore, having properly set out their condition in this matter, he also teaches us the state of the rest. For in the following verse he directs his speech to those who are most affected by the care of these things, and especially to those who let out their labour for them, adding also mention of the lack of other things which are most necessary for living pleasantly.

[2] The first word הובישׁ <i>Hobbishu</i> has the same form as הובישׁ <i>Hobish</i> in the preceding verse, which our people render <i>dried up</i>, and in the margin <i>put to shame</i>, since it admits both meanings, and it detracts nothing from the sense of this passage, whichever you take. But the same R. Tanchum, whom we mentioned as having noted this in that place, says that here it undoubtedly has the sense of <i>shame and confusion</i>. Nevertheless the LXX take it in the other sense, namely that in which it is commonly read and divided, since they render ἐξηράνθησαν γεωργοί (<i>the cultivators withered</i>), as does the printed Arabic, which follows them: <i>the cultivators have dried up</i>, which certainly gives a harsh sense and seems to need correction. Cyril divides it in another way, so that according to him ἐξηράνθησαν is merely a repetition of what was said in the preceding words, namely <i>they</i>, those previously mentioned, <i>have dried up</i>; and a new clause, joined with the following verb, γεωργοὶ θρηνεῖτε κλαυσατε (<i>cultivators, lament, weep over the possessions</i>), forms the sentence, as we shall soon see. Yet there are others, both of our time and of earlier centuries, who, in my own judgment, quite nicely take the verb in the sense of being ashamed, governed by the noun <i>cultivators</i>. They differ, however, in its mood and tense: some explain it in the past tense and, like the Vulgate, render it <i>the cultivators have been confounded</i>, to which the Syriac agrees, taken from the Latin translation, <i>the cultivators have blushed</i>. And modern interpreters, Junius and Tremellius, <i>are ashamed</i>, and others in the same sense, <i>are ashamed</i> or <i>the cultivators are put to shame</i>. Others prefer to take it in the imperative mood, as the Chaldean paraphrast, בהיתו אכריא <i>be ashamed, cultivators</i>, and the Arabic manuscript, <i>be shamed, O cultivators</i>, since the form admits either. Kimchi notes that this verb in the imperative mood is the same as the following צווי, which also pleases various others no less than our people in the latest interpretations, as most suitable and emphatic: <i>Be ashamed</i>, or as the interlinear glosses have it, <i>you cultivators, be struck with shame</i>. But just as either way of interpreting fits the form of the verb best, so neither breaks the sense: both express the magnitude of the calamity by which they were overwhelmed or were about to be, which ought rightly to provoke nothing but great mourning. If the past tense is taken, it indicates what has already been done; if the imperative mood, what ought rightly to be done by them in those circumstances, as Kimchi says: כי ראוי לכנס שתבושן <i>because it is fitting that you be filled with shame, since all labour and work in the field is in vain</i>; as is made clear from what follows. The ones to whom or about whom he speaks here are אכרים <i>Iccarim</i>, in Chaldean אכריא <i>Iccarya</i>, in Syriac <i>Acare</i>, and in the Arabic manuscript أكارين <i>Accarin</i>; all these come from the same root <i>Acar</i>, and all signify, as Kimchi says, עוברי השדות, cultivators of the fields, or, as R. Tanchum says, الأكارين, ploughmen or farmers, who plough or till the land. The singular אכר <i>Iccar</i> occurs in Jeremiah 51:23, which Abu Walid explains as אכר והו אלדי יקלב אלארץ באלהראר, <i>a farmer, that is, one who turns the land by ploughing</i>.
