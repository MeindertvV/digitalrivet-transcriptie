[1] This promise or privilege is made plain by what Abraham says in the parable (<i>Luc. 16, 6.</i>), as if from heaven, to the rich man in hell: <i>between us and you a great gulf is fixed</i>, so that people cannot pass from one side to the other. We suspect that this change was made in the Syriac copies, both printed and manuscript, through the mistake of a single letter, which can easily happen. Some early scribe wrote ↄ ḃ for yod and ḥet, and so wrote <i>Neebarun</i>, <i>they will pass</i>, in place of <i>Neemarun</i>, <i>they will dwell</i>. Since this gives a suitable sense, I think it came about that no one afterwards suspected the error when reading. It is also possible that the author at first rendered it thus, and was persuaded that it meant the same as <i>they shall not pass through that place</i>, or <i>they shall not dwell</i>, or <i>they shall have no liberty to fix a dwelling in it</i>, just as in fact they will have no strangers or sojourners among the faithful in the triumphant Church. That these words may be taken this way is also probable from the particle עוד [<i>ed</i>], <i>no more</i>, or <i>at all</i>, which also gets around the exception to which the other view seems liable, the view of those who apply it to the true Church still on earth, though militant, because it contains impious and profane people mixed in with its true members, even though these are not members but mere outsiders. But since there is no doubt at all that the divine promises pertain also to the church that is on earth, I think it will not be out of place to join them here with others, and to hold that both prophets had regard to it, as a sharer in the promises made in this place, which will be fulfilled <i>u) in the militant Church, which afterwards will become triumphant.</i> Here on earth strangers will not prevail; but there in heaven there is no trace at all of assailants and invaders.
