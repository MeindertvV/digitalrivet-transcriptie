Trem. <i>it will strip her bare.</i> The Chaldee, supposing that the stripping of the bark is hinted at here, renders it מקלף קלף [<i>mqlp qlp</i>] <i>by stripping, stripped her</i>. But Bochart is the authority that this need not be understood as <i>stripping bare</i>: he thinks the expression is sufficient if the tree is stripped of its leaves, fruits and flowers, even though the bark is left whole. The Seventy have ἐξεύνησεν αὐτὴν [<i>exeunēsen autēn</i>], <i>searching, it searched her</i> (as the printed Arabic also has, <i>searched by searching</i>, that is, as the Latin translator rendered it, <i>it searched her diligently</i>). This gave some people an opportunity to conjecture that they had once read in the Hebrew text, as we do today, חשיף [<i>chsyp</i>], but rather חפשי חשש [<i>chpsy chsh</i>], <i>Caphas Chaphafah</i>, with the letters transposed, since they noticed that <i>Caphas</i> more often has the sense of <i>searching</i>. But since everyone knows that <i>Caphas</i> admits both meanings, <i>of uncovering and of stripping bare</i>, I do not see why, since the word agrees with <i>Cafaph</i> in one sense, it should not be allowed to agree with it in the other as well. Moreover, the difference between these meanings is not so great that one does not include the other: things are opened and stripped bare so that they may be investigated, and so the locust can be said to have stripped the fig tree bare in order to search out the edible parts of it that would be of use to it. The Syriac alone has, without repeating the word, ܓܝܪܐ [<i>gyra</i>], <i>it tore her apart</i>.
