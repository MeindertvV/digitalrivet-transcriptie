[1] e) Duacenus. d) Drusius. a Calt. e) Mercerus. Tarnov. f) Munster. Pagnin. Tigur. The words that follow, הילילו כורמים [Helilu Ceremin], are rendered "wail, vinedressers." First, the verb Helilu comes from ילל [jalal, to howl], and it has the same form and conjugation as the preceding Hobishu [הובישו]. So it makes no difference whether you translate it as a past tense or as an imperative, and for that reason many take it in the same way as the word before. Hence some prefer the past tense, as the Vulgate does, "the vinedressers have wailed," and the Syriac, ܝܠܝܠܘ ܕܟܪܡܐ [Waalelu carme], which the Latin interpreter renders "the vinedressers have wailed"; Junius and Tremellius, "the vinedressers wail." Others prefer the imperative mood, as the Chaldee הילילו כרמים [Hililu carmim] does, following the common translation, "wail, you tillers of the vineyards," and Mercerus, "wail," etc. (although some prefer to take both this word and the preceding one as past tenses, "the farmers are put to shame, the tillers of the vineyards have wailed," since the forms do not oppose it), and likewise the printed Arabic and the manuscript Arabic, ولولوا يا كرميں [walwalu ya karmin], "wail, vinedressers." Indeed, it must be taken this way together with the preceding word, as Kimchi notes, and it is the view of more recent interpreters who read it so. Only the Greek differs from the others here, and for this reason the printed Arabic, following it, renders the first verb as a past tense, ἐξηρξύθησαν [exērxuthēsan], and the second as an imperative, θρηνεῖτε [thrēneite], "wail." The people the prophet addresses, or of whom he speaks, are כורמים [Ceremin], that is, עובדי הכרמים [Obede buccermim], as Kimchi explains, those who are employed in arranging, planting and tending vineyards. So the Chaldee has מפלחי כרמיא [Mephalechi carmya], "cultivators of vineyards," and the Syriac ܟܪܡܐ [Corme] and the manuscript Arabic ܟܪܡܢ [Carramin], words of the same root, sound and meaning as the Hebrew. For it signifies vinedressers, as the Vulgate translated it, and nearly all recent interpreters with it, except the Greek and the printed Arabic, which have κλήματα [klēmata], "branches," and this Arabic word مواشي [Cattel], "cattle." To reconcile this with the Hebrew text, some conjecture that in place of Ceremin, "vinedressers," they read Ceramin, which means "vines," and that the Greek had κλήματα, which denotes "shoots" of the vine.
