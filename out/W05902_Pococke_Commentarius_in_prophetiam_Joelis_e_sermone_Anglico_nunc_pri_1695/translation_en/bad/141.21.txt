The Vulgate renders it by the general term <i>animalis</i>, while the Chaldee and the Syriac have <i>Beira</i> and <i>Beiro</i>, which corresponds well to the Hebrew <i>behemah</i>. Syriac lexicographers explain that it means <i>beasts, cattle</i>, among living creatures [illegible, about twelve letters] <i>which feed on grass, or of which some are edible and some are needed for service.</i> Here, however, there is a great difference between the LXX, whom the printed Arabic also follows, and the Hebrew text and its interpreters. For the LXX have <i>τί ἀποθήσομεν ἑαυτοῖς</i> [ti apothēsomen heautois], which in Latin, according to Jerome, reads <i>quid reponamus nobis?</i> <i>(what shall we lay up for ourselves?)</i> Since this can by no means be reconciled with the Hebrew text as it is held today, some who were willing to examine the matter more closely have conjectured that they once read differently: namely, for <i>behemah</i> <i>n)</i> <i>behemmah</i> or <i>bahem</i>, and for <i>neenchah</i>, which comes from <i>anach</i>, <i>genuit</i> (he groaned), <i>o)</i> <i>nnechah</i>, as if it came from <i>nach</i>, <i>statuit, collocavit</i> (he set, placed). But even so the Hebrew text does not agree with the Greek version, and this is shown by the fact that these very reconcilers, being less content with such, finally take refuge in a different reading of the Greek version, which they suppose the old copies had otherwise than the present ones: namely, where the present copies have <i>ἑαυτοῖς</i> [heautois] <i>ipsis</i>, it should (according to some) be read more correctly <i>αὐτοῖς</i> [autois], <i>illis</i>, or (according to others) <i>ἐν αὐτοῖς</i> [en autois], <i>in illis</i>. If the latter is approved, the pronoun must refer to the <i>p)</i> granaries and storehouses mentioned above, which lie desolate and destroyed, since there was no longer any use for them: <i>What shall we lay up in them, or what have we that we could store in them?</i> If the former, it must refer to the herds, the perplexed herds of oxen and the desolate flocks of sheep, and then <i>behem</i>, <i>to me</i>, could be taken to stand for <i>q)</i> <i>lahem</i>, <i>to them</i> or <i>for them</i>, as if you were to say: <i>r)</i> <i>where shall we store up for those beasts?</i> (where shall we find food for them?) Thus <i>l)</i> Jun., Trem. <i>m)</i> ... <i>and several</i> ... <i>not to be read as two clauses of question.</i> <i>r)</i> Mercer, Drus., Cappel. <i>o)</i> Mercer. <i>p)</i> That is, in the granaries, <i>ἀποθῆκαι</i> [apothēkai], Drus. <i>q)</i> Cappel. <i>r)</i> Mercer. Thus one conjecture hangs upon another, so there is no need to inquire anxiously which of these is most correct, or whether any of them is true, since the usual reading of the Hebrew text presents us with a clearer sense, and one far more suitable, which we rightly embrace. Certainly it matters as little for us to investigate how they read as whether they had a just reason for reading thus. But since not one kind of animal but several kinds have been struck and afflicted by these plagues, the Prophet also takes pains to show them specifically; therefore he first adds <i>the herds of oxen</i>, <i>בקר</i> [baqar] ... <i>Nabocu</i> [nevokhu] <i>are perplexed</i>, from the root <i>בוך</i> [buch], which in its simple form is not in use, but the words formed and derived from it, wherever they occur, signify being entangled and confused, as in <i>Exod. 14,3</i>: <i>נבוכים הבית בארץ</i> [nevukhim habbayit ba'arets], where our people use the equivalent word <i>are entangled in the land</i>, which Ainsworth retains, but adds that literally it means <i>are perplexed</i>. So in <i>Esther 3, last verse</i>, the city of <i>Shushan</i> was <i>נבוכי</i> [nevukhi], <i>perplexed</i>, whence comes the noun <i>מבוכה</i> [mevukhah], <i>perplexity</i>, <i>Isa. 22,5</i> and <i>Mic. 7,4</i>. Nor is this sense little confirmed by the use of this root in the Arabic language, in which from the root <i>بوك</i> [bawaca], <i>Baca</i>, and <i>أبك</i> [anbaca], <i>Enbaca</i>, people are said to be (when) <i>u)</i> ... <i>their minds (or counsels) are so confused that they find no way to extricate themselves</i> (or cannot bring themselves to decide) <i>whether to go on or to go back.</i> To this also Kimchi looks, when he says that this word signifies <i>בלבול</i> [balbul], <i>confusion</i> (perplexity) ... when a man is perplexed in his mind (or thoughts) and does not know what he should do. So there is no doubt that both our people and the other interpreters do right, who ascribe the sense of perplexity to the word as it is read in Hebrew today. <i>w)</i> The LXX, however, have <i>ἐλαυσαν βοσκία βοῶν</i> [elausan boskia boōn], <i>the herds of oxen have gone</i> (mourned), whence it occurred to the learned that they did not read <i>nabocu</i> from <i>boc</i>, to be perplexed, but <i>nibcu</i> from <i>בכה</i> [bakah], <i>he wept</i>.
